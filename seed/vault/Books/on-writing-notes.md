Half memoir, half manual, and the seam between them is deliberate: King wants you to see that the craft advice came out of a life of doing the work badly and then less badly, not out of a theory.

The toolbox chapter is the part I return to. King's claim is that you carry a small number of tools on the top level — vocabulary you already own, grammar, the paragraph as the true unit of composition — and that the writer's job is knowing which one to reach for, not acquiring more. It is the least glamorous chapter and the most useful practical writing advice I have read anywhere.

## The three lines that changed how I work

- "Write with the door closed, rewrite with the door open." A first draft has one reader. Revision has all of them. That is a cleaner statement of [[Revision Is Re-Seeing]] than most craft books manage in a chapter.
- "2nd draft = 1st draft − 10%." A quota for cutting, which turns [[Kill Your Darlings, Gently]] from a mood into arithmetic you can actually check.
- 2,000 words a day, doors shut, no exceptions on holidays. His argument for [[The Daily Quota]] is not that the words are good but that the habit keeps the story alive in your head between sessions.

He is also blunt that his early novels got finished because he refused to abandon them at the boring middle, which is the strongest case I know that [[Finishing Is a Separate Skill]] independent of talent. Plenty of people write better sentences than King. Very few finish sixty books.

Where I part ways: his hostility to outlining is temperamental, presented as universal. It works for a writer who discovers plot by writing. It does not follow that plotting is dishonest.
