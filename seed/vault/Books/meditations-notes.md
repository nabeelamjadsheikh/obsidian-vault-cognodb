The most important fact about this book is that it was never a book. It is a private notebook, written in Greek by a Roman emperor on campaign, addressed to nobody, with a title (*To Himself*) supplied by someone else. Read it as a treatise and it seems disorganised and repetitive. Read it as what it is and the repetition becomes the method.

He writes the same handful of things over and over because he keeps needing them again. That is the honest shape of a practice, and it is the strongest argument I know for [[Journaling as Practice, Not Record]]: the entries are not preserving insight, they are re-installing it before the next hard morning.

Book II opens with the line about the people he will meet today — meddling, ungrateful, arrogant — and ends by reminding himself they act that way through ignorance of good and evil, so he cannot be harmed by them. That is [[The Dichotomy of Control]] applied at 6 a.m. to a specific dread, not stated as doctrine.

The passages that do the most work for me:

- **The view from above.** He repeatedly zooms out to the whole earth, the whole of time, Vespasian's court and everyone in it now dust. Grief and ambition both shrink under it, which is the entire technique in [[The View From Above]].
- **Amor fati, in its Roman form.** "Everything harmonises with me which is harmonious to thee, O Universe." Less cheerful than Nietzsche's version and more like a soldier's, but the same move as [[Amor Fati]].
- **Book I.** The catalogue of debts to his teachers and family. Sixteen paragraphs of gratitude with no philosophy in them, written by the most powerful man alive.

Hays' translation is the readable one. Long's is the one people quote. Neither is wrong.
