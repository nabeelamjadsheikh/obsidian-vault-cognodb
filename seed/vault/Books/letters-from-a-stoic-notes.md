124 letters to Lucilius, written in Seneca's last years while he was easing himself out of Nero's court. The form is the reason they still work: philosophy delivered as correspondence has to argue with a real situation — a friend's illness, a noisy bathhouse under the apartment, a bad dinner party, a shipwreck — rather than with an abstraction.

The recurring drill is rehearsal. Letter 91, on the fire that destroyed Lyon, is the clearest statement: nothing should be unexpected to us, and the mind should be sent ahead into every misfortune. That is [[Premeditatio Malorum]] argued from a news event rather than from first principles, which is exactly why it lands.

Letter 18 is the other one I return to, where he tells Lucilius to set aside days of coarse food and a hard bed and ask, "Is this what I feared?" His point is not asceticism for its own sake — he was extraordinarily rich and knew how bad that looked — but calibration, and it is the source of everything in [[Voluntary Discomfort]].

> Set aside a certain number of days during which you shall be content with the scantiest and cheapest fare, with coarse and rough dress, saying to yourself the while, "Is this the condition that I feared?"

On the passions he is at his sharpest. His account of anger as something that begins as a small assent and then feeds on its own momentum is a better description than the modern cliché of "letting off steam"; I unpacked it in [[Anger Is a Reinforcing Loop]]. The essential complaint about how we spend our days runs through the letters too, but he makes it properly in [[Notes on On the Shortness of Life]].

The hypocrisy charge is fair and mostly uninteresting. He wrote about frugality while banking a fortune, and he knew it — Letter 87 is him arguing with himself about the carriage he travels in.
