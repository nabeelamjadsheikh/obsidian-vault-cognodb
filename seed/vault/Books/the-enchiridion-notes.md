Epictetus wrote nothing. Everything we have was taken down by his student Arrian, and the *Enchiridion* — "the thing in your hand", a manual — is Arrian's condensation of the longer *Discourses*. That editorial history explains the tone completely. It reads like drill instructions because it is a set of drills, extracted from a teacher's lectures by someone who wanted a portable version.

Chapter 1 is the whole system: some things are up to us and some are not. Opinion, impulse, desire, aversion — ours. Body, property, reputation, office — not ours. Everything downstream is application, and the compressed argument in [[The Dichotomy of Control]] never improves on that paragraph.

What the handbook loses in the compression is the argument. The *Discourses* show him reasoning, joking, losing patience with a student; the manual just issues commands. So the two should be read together, and the parts of his ethics that make the least sense in isolation are the ones about station and duty, which is why I wrote [[Epictetus on Roles]] separately.

## The drills that actually transfer

- Chapter 5: people are disturbed not by things but by their opinions about things. Say it before saying anything else about a bad morning.
- Chapter 8: don't demand events happen as you wish; wish them to happen as they do.
- Chapter 33: fix a character for yourself and keep it whether alone or with others — his version of the claim that [[Virtue as a Skill]] is trained, not felt.
- The broken jug and the dead child. This is the passage that gets him accused of coldness, and the accusation misreads him; I argued that out in [[Stoicism Is Not Suppression]].

> Seek not that the things which happen should happen as you wish; but wish the things which happen to be as they are, and you will have a tranquil flow of life.
