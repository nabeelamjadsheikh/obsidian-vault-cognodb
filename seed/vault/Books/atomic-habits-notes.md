The useful half of this book is about designing environments. The other half is repackaged behaviourism with better branding, and you can skim it without loss.

What earns its place is the argument that self-control is mostly a property of rooms rather than people. Disciplined people are not resisting temptation all day; they have arranged their lives so the temptation is not in the room. Clear's practical moves — put the guitar on a stand, keep the phone charging in the hallway, add friction in units of twenty seconds — are cheap, testable, and they work on me in a way that resolutions never have.

The second thing I took from it is the primacy of showing up. His "never miss twice" rule and the two-minute version of a habit both concede that the size of the action matters less than its non-negotiability, which is the same insight behind [[The Daily Quota]] and, in a harsher register, behind [[Voluntary Discomfort]] — you build the identity by doing the small unpleasant thing on the day you don't want to.

He is also right, though he doesn't put it this way, that [[Motivation Follows Action]]. The whole habit-stacking apparatus exists to get you moving before you have consulted your feelings about it.

## Where it thins out

The four laws are a marketing frame more than a theory; the underlying cue-craving-response-reward loop is decades old and Clear knows it. The identity chapters ("become the type of person who…") are the weakest — they gesture at self-concept research that is much shakier than the environment-design material. And the book badly under-discusses habits that are hard because the work itself is hard, not because the cue is missing.
