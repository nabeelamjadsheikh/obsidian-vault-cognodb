Ahrens set out to reconstruct Luhmann's workflow for people who write, and along the way makes a claim that reorganised how I work: the writing problem is really a note problem. Nobody sits down to a blank page and thinks. They sit down to *assemble*, and if there is nothing to assemble from, the blank page is doing the impossible.

## The three-box distinction

The operational core of the book is that notes come in kinds and mixing them destroys the system. Fleeting notes are captures with a shelf life of a day. Reference notes are short, in-your-own-words records of what a source said. Permanent notes are your claims, written in full sentences, understandable in six months with no source in front of you. Only the third kind is an asset, and the discipline of [[Literature Notes Versus Permanent Notes]] is what keeps the box from silting up with quotes.

Two rules make it work. Each note holds one idea, because a note holding three cannot be linked to precisely — that is the case for [[Atomic Notes]]. And every new note must be filed *behind* an existing one, so the act of filing forces a comparison and often produces the connection you were not looking for.

## What's overstated

The productivity framing is oversold — the "Luhmann published 70 books" figure gets deployed as if the box did it. And roughly a third of the book is a tour of psychology research (ego depletion among it, which has aged badly) used to justify practices that stand on their own.

Still the best single entry point. Ahrens is at his most convincing when explaining why the box talks back rather than merely storing — the property I chased down in [[Luhmann's Second Memory]] — and reading him is why I stopped treating my own box as a filing cabinet, which is the reasoning I set down in [[Why I Keep a Zettelkasten]].
