Meadows drafted this in 1993 and never published it; it came out posthumously in 2008 and is now the standard entry point, deservedly. The reason it sticks where the technical literature does not is that she teaches structure through stories rather than equations — the bathtub, the thermostat, the car dealership guessing next month's orders.

She starts with the smallest possible object: a tub with a tap and a drain. From that she gets the whole grammar of [[Stocks and Flows]], including the two facts people most reliably get wrong — that a stock can keep rising while its inflow is falling, and that stocks create delay, which is why systems overshoot.

Then structure. Every behaviour over time is generated by some arrangement of [[Feedback Loops]], and her "system zoo" of archetypes is a catalogue of the arrangements that keep recurring: drift to low performance, escalation, success to the successful, policy resistance where every actor pulls the stock toward their own goal and it stays put.

The chapter everyone remembers is the ranked list of intervention points, from parameters at the bottom to paradigms at the top. It is worth reading against [[Leverage Points]], her own essay, because the book version is softer and more honest about how often the high-leverage places are exactly the ones nobody has authority over.

Her worked example of shared resources — the commons — is the best short treatment I have read, and it makes the structural point that [[The Tragedy of the Commons]] is a missing-feedback problem rather than a morality problem. The herders are not villains. The signal from the pasture never reaches them.

> There is no single, legitimate boundary to draw around a system. We have to invent boundaries for clarity and sanity; and boundaries can produce problems when we forget that we've artificially created them.
