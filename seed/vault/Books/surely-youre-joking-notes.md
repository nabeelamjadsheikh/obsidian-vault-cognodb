A book of anecdotes transcribed from drumming sessions with Ralph Leighton, which is why it reads like a man talking and why the structure is a mess. It is also, buried in chapter after chapter of safe-cracking and samba bands, the most persuasive account of a research temperament I have read.

The wobbling plate is the whole thing. Post-war, burnt out, unable to work, he decides physics is no longer fun and resolves to play with it instead — no obligation, no importance. Someone throws a plate in the Cornell cafeteria, he notices the medallion rotates faster than the wobble, works out the ratio because it amuses him, and Bethe asks what the point is. There is no point. That work on rotation led to the electron orbit diagrams, and to the Nobel. The lesson is not "play leads to prizes"; it is that the prize came only when he stopped optimising for prizes, which is the argument in [[Play Is Serious Work]].

Underneath the stories is a consistent claim about what drives him: not ambition, not competition, but the itch of not knowing. He picks locks, learns Portuguese, paints, decodes Mayan glyphs, tests ant navigation with sugar and pencil trails on his bathroom floor. Nothing is beneath the same attention. If you want a case study for [[Curiosity as the Engine]], it is here, at book length.

Two other threads I keep pulling on:

- The Brazil chapter, where students recite refractive index definitions and cannot say what happens looking through water, is a devastating field report on learning without understanding.
- The comedy is not a break from the seriousness. His clowning and his physics come from the same place, which is the point of [[Seriousness and Whimsy]].

And the wobbling plate only happened because he had *no* resources and no mandate — the situation described in [[Constraints Breed Creativity]], arrived at accidentally.
