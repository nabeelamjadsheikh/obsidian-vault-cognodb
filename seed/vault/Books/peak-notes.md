Ericsson wrote this book partly out of irritation. Thirty years of careful research on violinists, chess players and memory athletes had been compressed by popularisers into a single round number, and *Peak* is his attempt to take the work back.

The 1993 paper it draws on is more careful than its reputation. It reports that the best violinists at the Berlin academy had accumulated more solitary practice than the good ones — a correlation, in a domain with clear standards, coaches and immediate feedback. It does not say ten thousand hours is a threshold, and it does not say talent is irrelevant. Everything wrong with [[The Ten Thousand Hours Myth]] was added later.

## What deliberate practice actually requires

Ericsson's definition is narrow on purpose: a well-defined task just beyond current ability, full attention, immediate informative feedback, and repetition with correction. Strip any one of those and you have experience, not practice. This is why [[Deliberate Practice]] is nearly impossible to run in fields without objective standards — management, most of writing — and why he is candid that the concept transfers poorly there.

The mechanism chapter is the one I underlined most. What improves is not general processing speed but stored, domain-specific mental representations: the chess master sees positions, not pieces. That is the substance behind [[Expertise Is Mostly Pattern Recognition]], and it explains the odd finding that experts are barely better than novices once you scramble the board.

> The reason that most people don't possess extraordinary physical capabilities isn't because they don't have the capacity, but rather because they're satisfied to live in the comfortable rut of homeostasis.

The prose is workmanlike and the anecdotes repeat. Read it as a correction, not as entertainment.
