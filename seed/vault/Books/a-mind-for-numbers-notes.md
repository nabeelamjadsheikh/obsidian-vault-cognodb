Oakley's credibility comes from her biography: she flunked her way through school maths, joined the army as a linguist, and retrained as an engineer in her twenties. The book is written by someone who had to reverse-engineer learning from the outside, which is why it is a practical bridge from the cognitive psychology literature to what to actually do on a Tuesday evening.

The central metaphor is the two modes of attention. Her pinball table — tight bumpers for concentrated work, wide bumpers for the loose associative state — is the reason [[Diffuse and Focused Modes]] stuck with me when the underlying default-mode-network research never did. Her advice follows directly from it: when you are stuck, stop. The stuckness is focused mode holding you in a wrong groove.

## The practical core

- Work in Pomodoros to attack procrastination at the level of process, not outcome. The timer removes the negotiation.
- Build chunks deliberately — solve a problem, then solve it again from blank paper, then again a day later, until the whole procedure collapses into one move, which is exactly the process described in [[Chunking]].
- Use [[Spaced Repetition]] instead of cramming, because the point is retrieval over time rather than exposure over time.
- Test yourself constantly. Recall on a walk, away from the book, is her stand-in for the lab finding that [[Desirable Difficulties]] produce durable learning even though they feel worse in the moment.

The honest weakness is the neuroscience gloss. Some of the brain talk is decorative and a few claims are firmer in the book than in the literature. But the study advice underneath is well-sourced, and pairing it with [[Learning How to Learn — Course Notes]] gives you the same material twice, in two formats, which is itself good practice.
