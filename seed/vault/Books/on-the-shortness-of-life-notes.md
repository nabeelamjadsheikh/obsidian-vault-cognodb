An essay to his father-in-law Paulinus, who ran Rome's grain supply, telling him to quit. That practical occasion keeps it from floating off into consolation-literature vagueness.

The thesis is a reversal in the first paragraph. It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it. Life is long enough, and a sufficiently generous amount has been given to us for the highest achievements if it were all well invested. The essay is therefore not about death at all — it is about who you let spend your time for you.

His accounting metaphor is relentless and, I think, correct. We guard property obsessively and give away hours to anyone who asks, because time feels free at the moment of spending. The people he attacks are not idlers but the busy: the ones enslaved by their own status, chasing clients and appointments, "engaged in making others' lives happy at the cost of their own." Read him now and the target is obvious, which is why [[The Attention Economy]] is really an old complaint with new instrumentation.

> It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it.

Two things follow. First, his argument that the busy man lives only in the present, unable to look back on a past he never gathered, makes memory the payoff of attention — and turns [[Attention Is a Moral Act]] into a claim about a whole life rather than a work session. Second, the awareness of finitude is instrumental here, not morbid; it is what makes the audit possible, which is the honest use of [[Memento Mori]].

The temperament matches the letters, condensed into forty pages — see [[Notes on Letters from a Stoic]] for the same man arguing at more comfortable length.
