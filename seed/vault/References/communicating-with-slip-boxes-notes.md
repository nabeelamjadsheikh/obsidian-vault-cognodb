Luhmann's own short account of his method, from a 1981 volume on university teaching. Nine pages, and it corrects most of what the internet says about him.

The framing is the surprise. He does not describe a filing system; he describes a *communication partner*. His premise is that communication requires a counterpart capable of producing something you did not put in, and he argues the slip box qualifies because its internal referencing produces combinations he never anticipated. That is the whole of [[Luhmann's Second Memory]] in his own words, and it is a stronger claim than the productivity literature makes on his behalf.

> As a result of extensive work with this technique a kind of secondary memory will arise, an alter ego with whom we can constantly communicate.

The mechanics are almost aggressively unglamorous:

- Fixed slips, no hierarchy, no topic ordering. Notes go behind whatever note they arrive next to, and numbering branches (21/3a, 21/3a1) to allow infinite insertion between any two existing cards.
- Because position carries no meaning, the entry point has to come from somewhere else. His register of keywords is deliberately thin — a few entries per term, chosen — since an exhaustive index would just reproduce the problem it was meant to solve. That constraint is the substance of [[The Index Problem]].
- Internal references are the real structure. Any card can point to any other, so an idea can sit in several arguments at once without being duplicated.

He is candid that this only pays off after years, and that the box's usefulness is proportional to how much you've argued with it. That patience requirement is the honest part, and the part I try to keep in view in [[Why I Keep a Zettelkasten]] whenever I am tempted to reorganise instead of write.
