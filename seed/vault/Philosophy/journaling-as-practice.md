*Meditations* has no reader. Marcus was not writing for us, or for posterity, or even really for his later self. He was doing reps. The repetition that makes the book tedious to read straight through — the same handful of doctrines restated dozens of times in slightly different words — is the entire evidence of what the object was for. Nobody restates a thing forty times for a reader. You restate it forty times because you keep needing it.

Once you see the journal as a piece of exercise equipment, a lot of advice about journaling stops making sense. Prompts, gratitude lists, beautiful notebooks, the anxiety about missing days — these are all artefacts of treating it as a record that ought to be complete and worth keeping.

## What changes if it is practice

- Nothing needs to be preserved. Entries can be thrown away without loss, because the value was in the writing, not the artefact.
- Repetition is the feature. If you wrote the same realisation last month and it did not take, write it again.
- Honesty gets cheaper, because there is no audience to manage.

This is the sharpest available case of [[Writing Is Thinking, Not Its Transcript]] — the thought is not transcribed into the journal, it is constructed there, which is why it will not form if you only intend to have it.

It is also the daily-scale instance of [[Philosophy as a Way of Life]]. The doctrine in [[Notes on Meditations]] is unremarkable; the practice of hammering it into a tired man at the end of a bad day is the whole achievement.

The distinction from finished work matters, and I have kept it separate in [[Notebooks Are Not Drafts]]. Confusing the two ruins both: the journal gets self-conscious and the drafts get sentimental.
