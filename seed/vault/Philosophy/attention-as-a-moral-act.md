You are, over any long enough window, the sum of what you have attended to. Not what you believed, not what you intended — what actually occupied the field. That is an empirical claim about how minds are built, and it has an ethical consequence that I find hard to escape: choosing where attention goes is choosing what kind of person you are assembling.

This reframes a lot of small decisions as large ones. Reading the argument in the replies is not a neutral use of eleven minutes. It is eleven minutes of practising a particular way of relating to other people, and the practice compounds. So does the alternative.

## Two directions it cuts

**Inward.** What you rehearse, you become fluent in. The Stoics knew this, which is why they made the journal a training device rather than a diary — see [[Journaling as Practice, Not Record]].

**Outward.** Attention is the substance of most obligations to other people. Listening to someone is not a precondition for caring about them; a good deal of the time it simply is caring about them. Which is why divided attention reads as contempt even when no contempt is intended.

The uncomfortable corollary is that [[The Attention Economy]] is not a productivity problem. Systems engineered to capture attention are engineered to allocate a moral resource on your behalf, and they do not share your objectives. That framing is what pushed me toward [[Solitude and the Input Diet]] — not as a wellness measure, but because I could not otherwise say who was deciding what I thought about.

None of this is new. It is the whole content of [[Philosophy as a Way of Life]], which held that the discipline was about the direction of the mind long before it was about propositions.
