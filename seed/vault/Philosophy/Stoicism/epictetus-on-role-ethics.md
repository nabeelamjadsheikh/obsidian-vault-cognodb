Epictetus's ethics are surprisingly unabstract. Asked how one should behave, he does not reach for a principle; he asks what you are. A son. A brother. A citizen. A teacher. Each role carries duties, and the duties are legible without needing a theory of the good to derive them from.

> Remember that you are an actor in a play, the character of which is determined by the playwright.

The metaphor is often read as fatalism about circumstance, and it partly is. But the more interesting half is about performance: your business is to play the part well, and playing it well has content you can actually check. A father who is never present is failing at something specific, and no amount of tranquillity compensates for it.

This is what stops [[The Dichotomy of Control]] from collapsing into indifference. Outcomes are not up to you; the discharge of your role is. The role tells you where to aim the effort you have conceded the results of. It also makes the whole thing testable, which is exactly what treating [[Virtue as a Skill]] requires — a craft needs a specification of good work, and roles supply it.

The *Enchiridion* compresses this into its terse handbook form; see [[Notes on The Enchiridion]] for the passages where the roles do the heavy lifting.

Where it gets uncomfortable: Epictetus takes the roles largely as given by nature and society, and some of them are not ones I would accept unexamined. But the underlying move survives that objection. Duties attach to relationships, relationships are concrete, and [[Attention Is a Moral Act]] follows almost immediately — the person in front of you has a claim on your attention that abstract humanity does not. [[Philosophy as a Way of Life]] is the same insight at the level of the whole discipline.
