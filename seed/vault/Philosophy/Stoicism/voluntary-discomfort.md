Seneca tells Lucilius to set aside a few days each month to eat the coarsest food and wear the roughest clothes, and then to ask himself: is this what I was afraid of? The point is empirical. Fear of discomfort is a forecast, and forecasts can be tested cheaply.

I run a small version of this. Cold showers for the last two minutes, one meal a day skipped on Wednesdays, the twenty-minute walk instead of the bus when it is raining. None of it is impressive and none of it is meant to be. The finding is consistent: the anticipation is reliably worse than the event, by a margin large enough to change how I plan.

## What it actually buys

It widens the band of conditions inside which I can still think clearly. Tired, cold, hungry, mildly inconvenienced — these used to be states in which my judgement quietly degraded without my noticing. Practising them on purpose, when nothing is at stake, means I recognise the degradation when it happens for real.

This is the same logic as [[Premeditatio Malorum]], moved from imagination into the body. Where the visualisation gives you a rehearsed thought, the discomfort gives you a rehearsed nervous system, and the second is harder to argue with at three in the morning. [[Negative Visualisation Versus Anxiety]] matters here too: the exercise has to have a defined end, or it stops being a drill and becomes a way of life you did not choose.

Treating it as [[Virtue as a Skill]] keeps it honest — these are reps, not moral achievements, and reps are boring by design. [[Memento Mori]] supplies the reason to bother: the capacity is only worth building because the hard days are coming regardless.
