Seneca's advice is to spend some part of a comfortable evening imagining exile, poverty, and the death of people you love. Stated flatly it sounds pathological. His defence, repeated across [[Notes on Letters from a Stoic]], is that the shock of misfortune comes largely from its novelty, and novelty is the one component you can remove in advance.

> He robs present ills of their power who has perceived their coming beforehand.

The mechanism is specific. You are not trying to feel the loss now. You are trying to move the event from the category of the unthinkable into the category of the thinkable, so that when it arrives your mind has somewhere to put it. This is why [[The Dichotomy of Control]] has to come first: the rehearsal only works if it ends with the recognition that the thing rehearsed was never yours, which is a conclusion, not a mood.

Done badly it becomes something else entirely. The failure mode is a rehearsal with no terminal state — you run the scenario, feel the dread, and run it again. I have separated that out into [[Negative Visualisation Versus Anxiety]] because the two look identical from outside and are opposite from inside.

There is also a gratitude effect that Seneca is honest about being a side benefit rather than the goal. Having imagined the house gone, the house is briefly visible again. [[Memento Mori]] is the same operation with the one loss that is certain rather than merely possible, which is why it produces conviction where the others produce only readiness.
