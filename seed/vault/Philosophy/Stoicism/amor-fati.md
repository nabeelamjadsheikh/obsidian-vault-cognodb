Not resignation, not gritted-teeth acceptance, but wanting the thing that happened. Marcus keeps returning to it in [[Notes on Meditations]] with the image of a fire that takes whatever is thrown on it and makes flame out of it. The obstacle becomes fuel; the interruption becomes the material.

I find this the hardest of the Stoic moves to take seriously, and I think that is because it is usually presented as an attitude when it is actually an inference. Here is the argument as I understand it: once an event has occurred, it has moved permanently into the column of things not up to you — see [[The Dichotomy of Control]]. Wishing it otherwise is caring about something over which you now have exactly zero purchase. The only remaining live question is what you do with the situation as it stands. Loving fate is what full attention to that question feels like from the inside.

## Where I get stuck

The version I can defend is thin: given that this happened, I will treat it as my material. The version Marcus seems to hold is thicker — the cosmos is rationally ordered, therefore what happened was in some sense right. I do not believe the physics, and [[What the Stoics Got Wrong]] is mostly about the cost of losing it.

The thin version still does real work. It cuts off the counterfactual loop, which is where most of my wasted hours go. And it pairs naturally with [[The View From Above]]: zoom out far enough and the ruined afternoon is one event in a very long sequence of events, most of which also failed to consult me. [[Philosophy as a Way of Life]] is the frame that makes this a drill rather than a belief.
