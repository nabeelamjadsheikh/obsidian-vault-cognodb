The Stoics call virtue a *techne* — a craft, like carpentry or medicine. Epictetus leans on the analogy constantly in the *Discourses*, and he is not being decorative. If courage is a skill rather than a possession, then a whole set of consequences follows that moralising cannot deliver.

## What the analogy commits you to

- **Drills exist.** You do not become a carpenter by admiring joinery. [[Voluntary Discomfort]] and the visualisation exercises are the drills; they are boring and repetitive and that is the point.
- **Plateaus are normal.** Nobody concludes they are a bad person for hitting a plateau in the workshop. They conclude the current practice has stopped producing adaptation.
- **Feedback is required.** This is where the analogy bites hardest, and it is the same requirement [[Deliberate Practice]] identifies for every other skill: without a signal telling you when you got it wrong, repetition just consolidates the error.
- **Coaches beat exhortation.** Epictetus's classroom is a gym, and his students are told they are performing badly at specific things, not that they are wicked.

The last one is why I think the pop-Stoic register — inspirational quote, stern jaw, no correction mechanism — misses the tradition entirely. Nobody improves at a craft by being told to try harder.

[[Epictetus on Roles]] supplies the syllabus: the drills are not generic, they are indexed to the roles you actually occupy, which is what makes it possible to say concretely what "doing it well" means this week. [[Philosophy as a Way of Life]] is the wider frame — the whole ancient enterprise looks more like a training regime than a curriculum.

And it explains [[Stoicism Is Not Suppression]] cleanly. A skilled carpenter is not one who suppresses the desire to cut badly. They are one who no longer has it.
