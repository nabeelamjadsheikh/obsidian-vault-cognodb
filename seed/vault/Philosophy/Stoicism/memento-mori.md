Seneca's complaint in [[Notes on On the Shortness of Life]] is not that life is short. It is that we spend it as if it were long, and that the spending is done by people who would not part with a coin as carelessly as they part with an afternoon.

I have stopped treating this as a mood and started treating it as a scheduling constraint. A mood produces solemnity and then evaporates by lunchtime. A constraint produces decisions. If there are perhaps two thousand more Saturdays, then an argument about a stranger's opinion online costs a measurable fraction of a Saturday, and the question of whether it is worth that has an answer.

> It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it.

The uncomfortable part is that the constraint binds hardest on the pleasant wastes rather than the obvious ones. Nobody needs to be told that doomscrolling is expensive. The harder case is the second reorganisation of the vault this month, or the fourth revision of a note nobody including me will read again — see [[Against the Perfect System]] for where that particular leak goes.

Paired with [[The View From Above]], it produces a stable reading rather than either extreme: cosmically small, personally finite, therefore worth choosing carefully. Paired with [[Amor Fati]], it removes the fantasy of the deferred life, the one that starts properly once conditions improve. There is no such life. There is this Tuesday, which is already underway, and which is the only unit of time I have ever actually been given.
