Seneca wrote a whole treatise on anger and his central observation is structural rather than moral: anger sustains itself by producing more of its own cause. You are wronged, you rehearse the wrong, the rehearsal produces fresh evidence of how badly you were wronged, which justifies more rehearsal. Nothing external needs to happen for the intensity to climb.

That is precisely the shape described in [[Feedback Loops]] — output feeding back as input with a positive sign. Once you see it, the classical advice stops sounding like moralising and starts sounding like control engineering. "Delay is the greatest remedy for anger" is not patience-as-virtue; it is inserting lag into a loop whose gain exceeds one, which is exactly the intervention [[Balancing and Reinforcing Loops]] predicts will work.

## What follows from the structure

- Venting is not a release valve. It is another pass around the loop, and it raises the gain.
- The intervention has to be early, because reinforcing loops are cheap to interrupt at the first pass and expensive at the tenth.
- The thing to interrupt is the rehearsal, not the feeling. This is the point of [[Stoicism Is Not Suppression]] — you are cutting the assent that feeds the next iteration, not clamping the output.

The loop also runs on a specific fuel: the belief that something owed to you was withheld. [[The Dichotomy of Control]] is the fuel-supply intervention. Almost everything I have ever been angry about turns out, on inspection, to be an item I had quietly filed as mine when it never was — the response, the credit, the ordinary courtesy of a stranger in traffic.

I am better at diagnosing this after the fact than during. That is the honest state of the practice.
