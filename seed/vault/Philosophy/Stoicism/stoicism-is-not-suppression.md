The pop version says: feel nothing, show nothing, be a rock. That is not the doctrine, and the confusion costs the tradition most of its useful readers.

Epictetus's model in the *Discourses* is cognitive. An impression arrives — *this is a disaster* — and between the impression and the emotion sits an act of assent. You endorse the impression, and the emotion follows from the endorsement. The Stoic intervention is at the assent, which is to say before the feeling exists, not after. "Wait for me a little, impression. Let me see what you are." That is a question, not a clamp.

## The practical difference

Suppression targets the output: you feel the anger, you hold your face still, and the anger keeps running underneath doing damage. The Stoic targets the input: you interrogate the judgement that someone has wronged you and treated the wrong as a catastrophe, and if the judgement dissolves there is nothing left to hold in.

The Stoics also concede a category the popular version forgets — the *propatheiai*, the involuntary first movements. The flinch, the flush, the jolt of grief. These are not up to you and were never claimed to be, which is entirely consistent with [[The Dichotomy of Control]].

Where this becomes concrete is [[Anger Is a Reinforcing Loop]]: anger is the emotion whose maintenance most obviously depends on continued assent, since it survives only by rehearsing its own justification. Cut the rehearsal and it starves.

I do not think the account is fully right — see [[What the Stoics Got Wrong]] on grief in particular. But it is at least a mechanism, which is more than the rock offers, and [[Epictetus on Roles]] shows how much practical ethics he builds on top of it.
