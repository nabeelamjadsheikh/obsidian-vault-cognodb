Epictetus opens with a sorting operation and never really moves past it. Some things are up to us — judgement, intention, the direction of attention — and some things are not: bodies, reputations, other people, the weather, the outcome of the interview. Everything that follows in [[Notes on The Enchiridion]] is downstream of that one cut.

What makes it more than a slogan is that the sorting is genuinely hard. Most of what feels like it is up to me is a mixture. Whether I finish the draft is partly effort and partly whether my daughter sleeps through the night. The Stoic answer is to keep splitting until you reach the part that is unambiguously yours — the effort, not the finishing — and to invest your caring there.

## Why the sorting is the whole practice

- Misplaced caring is the diagnosis for almost every kind of distress the Stoics name.
- The remedy is never "care less"; it is "care about the correct object".
- The exercises are all training for this one discrimination under pressure.

[[Premeditatio Malorum]] is the rehearsal version of this — you practise the sorting on a hypothetical before you have to do it live. [[Amor Fati]] is the version applied backwards, to things that have already resolved into the not-up-to-me column permanently.

The most common misreading is that this licenses detachment from the world. It does not. Epictetus keeps his job, his students, his obligations. As [[Stoicism Is Not Suppression]] argues, the dichotomy is about where you locate the good, not about how much you engage. You can throw yourself entirely into a project whose outcome you have already conceded to fortune. In fact that is the only way to throw yourself in without flinching.
