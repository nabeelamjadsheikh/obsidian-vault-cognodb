Marcus does this constantly: he pulls the camera back until Rome is a dot, the emperor is one of a long line of forgotten emperors, and the thing currently ruining his morning has to compete for space with the whole of recorded history. [[Notes on Meditations]] collects the passages; they are among the few places where his prose stops being terse and starts being genuinely strange.

The exercise is a deliberate change of scale, and scale is the variable that panic gets wrong. An unanswered email occupies the same subjective volume as a bereavement if you never zoom out. The view from above is a calibration procedure — it does not tell you the problem is unimportant, it tells you how important it actually is relative to everything else that is also true.

Two things make it work rather than becoming a nihilism generator:

1. It is temporary. You go up, you get the reading, you come back down and do the work.
2. It is paired with re-engagement. Marcus goes up in order to return to his duties without resentment, not to excuse himself from them.

Run it too long and you get the corrosive version where nothing matters because everything is small. [[Memento Mori]] is the corrective, because it reintroduces urgency: the afternoon is finite even if it is cosmically insignificant, which is exactly why it should be spent well.

This is also the clearest case of [[Attention Is a Moral Act]] in the Stoic corpus — the practice is nothing but a deliberate reallocation of attention, performed on purpose, with a known effect. And once the reading comes back small, [[Amor Fati]] becomes considerably easier to mean.
