Ancient philosophy was not primarily a body of propositions. It was a set of spiritual exercises — meditations, memorisations, examinations of conscience, dialogues with a teacher — aimed at transforming how a person sees and lives. The doctrines existed to support the exercises, not the other way round.

Reading it as pure theory is an artefact of the modern university, which has a strong institutional preference for things that can be examined in writing. An exercise cannot be examined in writing. A doctrine can. So the doctrines were promoted, the exercises were demoted to biography, and two thousand years of practice became a reading list.

You can see the seam clearly in Marcus. Treated as theory, *Meditations* is a mediocre restatement of school Stoicism with nothing original in it. Treated as a training log, it is remarkable — a man doing his reps in the dark, badly, repetitively, for decades. Which reading is correct is settled by the object itself; see [[Journaling as Practice, Not Record]].

## Why this matters practically

If philosophy is theory, the question is *do I believe it*, and the answer changes nothing about Tuesday. If it is a way of life, the question is *am I practising it*, which has an answer you can check and a failure mode you can fix. That is the [[Virtue as a Skill]] framing, and it is the only one under which the ancient texts read as instructions rather than opinions.

It also relocates the ethics. [[Attention Is a Moral Act]] is not a conclusion of the system; it is closer to its operating principle, because every exercise is finally an exercise in the direction of the mind.

The honest caveat is in [[What the Stoics Got Wrong]] — the practice survives the loss of the metaphysics, but not undamaged.
