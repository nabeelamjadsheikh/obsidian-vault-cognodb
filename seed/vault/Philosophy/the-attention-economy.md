The phrase is usually used loosely, but it is worth taking the stock-and-flow language literally, because attention behaves exactly like a stock. It has a finite level. It refills slowly and at a rate you do not control. It drains through several taps at once, and the drains are much larger than the fill.

In Donella Meadows's vocabulary the interesting question is never the stock itself but the loops around it. Feed products contain a reinforcing loop with an unusually high gain: engagement produces data, data improves targeting, targeting produces engagement. Nothing in that loop references whether you are better off. There is no balancing loop on the outside except the one you install yourself, which is willpower, which is the weakest possible controller.

## Why the framing matters

Called a distraction problem, it invites a distraction solution — try harder, be more disciplined. Called a systems problem, it invites the correct question: where is the intervention point? Almost always it is structural rather than behavioural. Delete the app rather than resolve to use it less. Change the physical availability rather than the intention.

That is the whole design of [[A Thirty-Day Input Experiment]] — I stopped negotiating with myself about usage and changed what was reachable, and the results were more decisive than any amount of resolve had been.

The ethical weight comes from [[Attention Is a Moral Act]]. If attention allocation is character construction, then an industry optimised to allocate it is an industry constructing character at scale, without a mandate and without a theory of what it should be building.

The positive programme is [[Solitude and the Input Diet]]: not abstinence, but a deliberate choice about what gets to be an input at all, made once, in advance, while I still have the capacity to make it.
