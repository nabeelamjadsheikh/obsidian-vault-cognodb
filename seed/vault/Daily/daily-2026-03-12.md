Reread *The Architecture of Complexity* on the strength of one remembered parable and came away thinking Herbert Simon explained about four things I had filed as facts about software.

The watchmakers: Tempus builds each watch as one thousand-piece assembly and must restart from nothing every time he is interrupted; Hora builds stable ten-piece subassemblies and loses only the current one. Hora finishes; Tempus goes out of business. I had this filed under modularity, which is true and much too small. Simon's claim is stronger and stranger — that this is why complex systems that *exist* are almost all hierarchic, because the non-hierarchic ones never survived assembly long enough to be observed. It is a selection argument, not an engineering preference. [[Hierarchy and Near-Decomposability]] is the note and it needed rewriting after tonight.

The other half is the near-decomposability point: within a subsystem, interactions are fast and strong; between subsystems, slow and weak. Which means you can analyse the short run ignoring the between, and the long run ignoring the within. That is not a modelling convenience, it is why analysis is possible at all.

The thing I had not connected: [[Bounded Rationality]] and [[Satisficing]] are the same argument pointed inward. If a system is only tractable because it decomposes, then a mind is only effective because it stops. Both are claims that adequacy under limits is the actual mechanism, not a compromise.

And the boundaries are chosen, not found. Simon is careful that near-decomposability is a property of a description at a timescale, which is [[System Boundaries Are Choices]] and is the thing people forget the moment they draw an architecture diagram.

One dark corollary: if you measure a subsystem by an intra-system metric, you optimise the strong fast interactions and quietly wreck the weak slow ones, which is [[Goodhart's Law]] with a mechanism attached rather than as a proverb.
