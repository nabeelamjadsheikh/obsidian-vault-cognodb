Walked through the old quarter for two hours with no route. It is a mess — streets that end, a market grown into three separate alleys, a square that is only a square because four buildings happened to stop there. And it is the best part of the city to be in.

Everything that makes it good would be destroyed by any attempt to rationalise it. Widen the streets for access, regularise the market for hygiene, give the square a function, and you get somewhere defensible and dead. [[Cities as Emergent Systems]] is the note; the walk was the evidence.

The reason is not aesthetic. The quarter works through a huge number of local adjustments made by people who each knew one small thing — where the sun falls at four, where the deliveries come, which corner floods. None of them had the plan, and the plan does not exist anywhere. This is what [[Legibility and Its Costs]] gets right: a planner must be able to see a system to manage it, and making it visible strips out precisely the local knowledge that made it work.

What kept nagging is that "the quarter" is not a fact about the buildings. It is a boundary I drew on a walk. Change the timescale and the boundary moves — over a century the market is the stable thing and the buildings are the flow. [[System Boundaries Are Choices]], and I keep drawing them where it is convenient and then reasoning as if I had found them.

The Hofstadter connection arrived on the way home and I am still not sure it is more than a rhyme. The quarter is shaped by people whose sense of where they live is itself shaped by the quarter — the level that does the shaping is also the level being shaped. [[Self-Reference in Systems]] covers the tame version; [[Strange Loops]] is the ambitious claim, and *Gödel, Escher, Bach* spends six hundred pages earning it.
