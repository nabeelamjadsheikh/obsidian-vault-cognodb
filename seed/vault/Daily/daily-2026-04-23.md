Spent the evening arguing myself out of the cheerful version of Stoicism, which I have been quietly practising for two years, and writing down what survives.

The objection that finally landed: if the only real good is virtue and everything else is indifferent, then the Stoic has a fully worked-out reason not to mind injustice done to other people, and several of them wrote as though they did not mind. The consoling reading — that this is about equanimity, not indifference — is available in the texts, and so is the harsh reading, and I have been choosing the consoling one because I like it, not because it is better supported. That is not philosophy, that is shopping.

What survives the objection, in my view:

**Role ethics.** The most defensible part, and the least quoted. Epictetus keeps grounding obligation in the specific roles a person occupies — son, citizen, teacher — which supplies exactly the concern for others that the indifference doctrine seems to remove. [[Epictetus on Roles]] is the note and it deserves more weight than I have given it.

**The distinction between judgement and event**, narrowly construed. Not "nothing outside you matters", which is false, but "your assent is the part you own", which is true and useful.

**The practices, detached from the metaphysics.** They work whether or not the cosmology does.

What does not survive: the version where you never grieve. [[Stoicism Is Not Suppression]] is the note I want to be true and it is doing apologetics in places — the texts really do say some hard things about the death of a child.

And I remain unsure about the exercises. [[Negative Visualisation Versus Anxiety]] is a real distinction in principle and in practice I have used premeditation as a licence to catastrophise for an hour and call it training.

[[Amor Fati]] is the one I would keep last. Loving what happens is either the deepest thing in the tradition or a very elegant way of never asking whether something should have happened, and tonight I could not tell which. More in [[Notes on The Enchiridion]].
