Did the Le Guin exercise on sentence length tonight — write a paragraph of a single unbroken sentence, then a paragraph where nothing exceeds seven words. Both were hard in ways I did not predict, and the second was worse.

Then I did the diagnostic she does not ask for and counted the sentence lengths in my last four essays. Median seventeen words, and — this is the part I have been avoiding — the *shape* is nearly identical every time. Medium, medium, medium, short one for emphasis, medium. It is a tic. I have been reaching for the same rhythmic gesture at the same structural moment for about two years, and because each instance reads fine, I never noticed the pattern.

> One of the things that make a writer's voice recognisable is the rhythm of the prose.

[[Sentence Rhythm Carries Meaning]] is the note I wrote about this and clearly did not internalise, because writing down that rhythm is meaning is not the same as hearing your own.

- [x] Counted lengths across four essays; produced an unflattering histogram
- [ ] Rewrite one paragraph three ways with deliberately different rhythms
- [ ] Read the next draft aloud before anyone else sees it

The uncomfortable question underneath: how much of what I call voice is just habit I have stopped hearing? [[Voice Is What Survives Translation]] argues voice is the part that persists through paraphrase, which suggests the short-sentence tic is not voice at all — it is a mannerism, and mannerisms are what a reader notices on the second essay.

The one place the tic is doing real work is at openings. A short declarative first line commits you to something, which is the argument in [[The First Sentence Is a Contract]], and I do that well.

Where it hurts is explanation. My longer sentences are longest exactly when I am least sure, which readers can feel, and that is the failure mode named in [[Plain Language Is a Form of Respect]] and diagnosed properly in [[The Curse of Knowledge]] — I get syntactically dense precisely when I have stopped modelling the reader.
