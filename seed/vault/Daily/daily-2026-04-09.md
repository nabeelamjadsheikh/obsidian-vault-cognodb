Reinstated the thousand words a day after six weeks off. Four days in and the shape of it is exactly what King says it will be, which I resent slightly.

Day one: 1,000 words, all bad in a specific way — hedged, throat-clearing, three paragraphs of setup before any claim. Day two: worse, and slower. Day three: unusable, and I nearly stopped, on the reasonable grounds that producing garbage on a schedule is not obviously better than producing nothing. Day four: six hundred words in the middle that are the best thing I have written this year, arrived at without warning.

That is the argument for [[The Daily Quota]] entire, and it cannot be made in advance. Three days of waste is the price of the fourth. Judging the practice on days one to three, I would have been correct and wrong.

- [x] 4/4 days at quota
- [x] No editing during the session, which was the hardest rule
- [ ] Keep going through the weekend; day five is where I usually quit
- [ ] Do not reread days one to three yet

> The scariest moment is always just before you start.

*On Writing* is right that the blank page is not really a page problem — [[The Blank Page Problem]] is a permission problem, and a quota solves it by removing the decision. I do not have to believe today's words are worth writing; I have to write a thousand of them.

The mechanism that matters most: the quota converts writing from an event into a rate. Days one to three were the cost of restarting a stalled flow, and what stops most of my projects is treating that cost as a verdict — [[Finishing Is a Separate Skill]] is the same muscle in a different position.

What the quota does *not* fix is structure. Four days of drafting has produced material and no shape, which is what [[Outlines Are Scaffolding, Not Blueprints]] predicts — the outline should come from the draft, not before it, but it does have to come.

The good six hundred words start with a sentence that commits to something, which is [[The First Sentence Is a Contract]] arrived at accidentally.
