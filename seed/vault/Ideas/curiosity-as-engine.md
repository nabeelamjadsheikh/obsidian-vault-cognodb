Feynman's story about the wobbling plate is the whole argument in one anecdote. Burned out, unable to work, he decided he would only play with physics — and someone threw a plate in the Cornell cafeteria, and he worked out the ratio of the wobble to the spin for no reason at all. That led to the rotation of electron orbits, and that led to the Nobel work. He is explicit that it came from doing something useless because it was fun. The rest of what I took from the book is in [[Notes on Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!]].

The reason curiosity beats discipline over long horizons is an accounting one. Discipline is a cost paid every morning out of a finite budget, and it must be renewed indefinitely. Curiosity is not a cost at all; it supplies its own energy, so the effort it produces is free. Over a week discipline wins easily. Over a decade it is not close.

The other half of Feynman is the honesty — the Cargo Cult Science lecture, where the demand is to bend over backwards to show how you might be wrong. That is the same faculty pointed at yourself: genuine curiosity about whether your idea is true rather than about whether it wins.

Three things that follow. Curiosity only survives in conditions where useless questions are permitted, which is the argument in [[Play Is Serious Work]]. Its absence has a symptom worth reading rather than fighting — the case in [[Boredom Is a Signal]]. And it is temperamentally incompatible with solemnity, since the wobbling plate is an unserious object and had to be taken seriously anyway, which is the pairing in [[Seriousness and Whimsy]].
