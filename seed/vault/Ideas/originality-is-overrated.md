Aiming at originality produces gimmicks. Aiming at usefulness produces work that, years later, people call original. The two aims feel similar from the inside and diverge completely in what they make you do.

Chasing novelty means the question in your head is *has this been done*. That question has one reliable answer available at low effort — do the thing nobody has done because it was not worth doing — and that is what most self-consciously original work is. It is legible as new precisely because it is not connected to anything.

Chasing usefulness means the question is *does this help*, and answering it drags you into a real problem, real constraints, and other people's prior attempts. The result usually turns out to differ from what came before, because your problem was specific and nobody else had exactly it. That difference is what originality actually is: a byproduct of engaging with something particular, never a target.

The theoretical backing is that nothing is new anyway. If ideas are recombinations, as in [[Creativity Is Combinatorial]], then originality is a statement about which combination and when, not about creation from nothing. And the reachable combinations are constrained by where you stand, per [[The Adjacent Possible]] — which is why simultaneous invention is so common and why nobody's originality was ever quite theirs.

The practical liberation is that copying stops being shameful. Deliberate imitation is how skill has always transmitted, which is the case in [[Imitation Is Apprenticeship]] — and the layer to copy is the architecture, since borrowed structure leaves your own work intact while borrowed mannerisms produce costume, per [[Steal the Structure, Not the Style]].

The tell for the failure mode is a piece whose main claim is that it is different. If that is the strongest thing you can say for it, you have a gimmick.
