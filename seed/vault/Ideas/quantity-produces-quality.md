The ceramics class story, from Bayles and Orland: the teacher split the class in two. One half would be graded on the single best pot they produced. The other half would be graded purely on weight of pots produced — fifty pounds an A, forty a B. At the end, the best pots came from the quantity group, without exception.

The quantity group spent the term making pots and learning from each failure. The quality group spent the term theorising about excellence and produced one grand, mediocre object.

I have watched this hold in writing, in design, in code. The mechanism is not mystical: attempts are the only source of information about what works, and a term of attempts contains more information than a term of planning. It also produces something planning never does — a large enough sample that any single failure stops feeling like a verdict.

The link to structured practice matters, because volume alone is not enough. Fifty pounds of pots with no attention to what went wrong is just fifty pounds of pots. The gains come when each attempt is examined, which is what [[Deliberate Practice]] adds. Volume creates the opportunities; correction converts them.

This is the fastest available route through the years described in [[The Taste Gap]], and Lamott's whole argument about permission is really the same claim from the emotional side: you cannot produce volume if each attempt has to be good, which is why the bad draft has to be allowed.

The other thing volume buys is compost. A large heap of abandoned attempts is not waste; it is the raw material that recombines later, which is what [[The Idea Compost Heap]] is for.
