Every craft tradition that reliably produced skilled people ran on copying. Painters copied in galleries for years. Composers transcribed. Franklin taught himself prose by reconstructing Spectator essays from notes days later and comparing his version to the original, sentence by sentence, and the comparison was the lesson.

Modern creative education mostly forbids this, on the grounds that you should find your own voice. The result is people with no technique searching for a voice they have no means to express.

What makes copying work is that it is **targeted**. Not admiring reproduction, but copying with a specific question: how does this transition work, why does this paragraph land, what is being withheld here. That is the difference between imitation as practice and imitation as plagiarism, and it is also what makes it satisfy the conditions in [[Deliberate Practice]] — a defined target, immediate comparison against a model, and correction.

The Franklin method is still the best version. Reconstruct from a skeleton, compare, and every difference is a diagnosis. You cannot get that signal from producing original work, because original work has no reference answer.

The layer you copy determines whether you learn anything. Copying surface mannerisms produces pastiche; copying architecture produces transferable skill, which is the argument in [[Steal the Structure, Not the Style]].

This is also the fastest way through the years described in [[The Taste Gap]], because it gives you something to measure against that is not your own vague dissatisfaction.

And it only feels shameful if you believe work should come from nowhere. Drop that and copying is simply how transmission works — see [[Originality Is Overrated]].
