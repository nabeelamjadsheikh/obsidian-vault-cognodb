I saved four thousand articles over six years. I have read maybe two hundred of them a second time and used perhaps thirty. The saving felt like intellectual activity and was, in fact, a way of not reading things while feeling like I had.

The diagnosis: collecting without a question is hoarding. The question is what makes a quote worth keeping, because the question is what makes it findable, comparable, and eventually useful. "This is interesting" is not a question. It is a feeling with no retrieval path attached.

What changed my practice was making the questions explicit and few. I keep a short list — six or seven at a time — of things I am actually trying to work out. When I read, I am reading against that list. A passage that answers, complicates or contradicts one of them gets kept, with a line saying which and how. Everything else, however good, goes past.

The counterintuitive result is that this made me keep **less** and use **far more**. It also made the keeping fast, since the note is one line and the line is the thinking.

Where the question comes from matters. Mine surface out of drafts, not out of reading — a piece I am struggling to finish generates a real question, which is why the running list of unfinished pieces in [[The Writing Inbox]] is the source of the questions rather than the other way round.

Two connected practices. The gathering is only half; the digestion is what makes it yours, which is Seneca's whole point in [[Keeping a Commonplace Book]]. And material kept against a live question naturally piles up next to other material about the same question, which is the productive mess described in [[The Idea Compost Heap]].
