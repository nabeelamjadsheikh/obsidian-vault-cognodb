The word means to look again, and I take that literally. Revision is not proofreading with ambition. Proofreading asks whether the sentences work. Revision asks whether this is the piece.

The test I use: after the draft cools for a week, I write down in one line what the piece is about, without looking at it. Then I read it. If the line and the draft disagree, no amount of sentence polishing helps — the draft is about something else and I should either change the draft or change my mind about the subject.

Stephen King's rule about cutting ten percent is downstream of this. Once you know the real subject, the cuts are obvious rather than painful, which is what [[Kill Your Darlings, Gently]] is really describing. I pulled the rest of it into Notes on On Writing.

Two mechanical aids:

- [[Read It Aloud]] catches rhythm and false emphasis, which are structural symptoms as often as they are sentence problems.
- Sketching the skeleton of the finished draft — one line per paragraph — exposes whether it has the load-bearing form described in [[The Shape of an Essay]], or whether the middle is three paragraphs of throat-clearing.

The distinction that matters: line editing makes a mediocre piece pleasant to read. Revision makes it a different, better piece. Most of us do the first and call it the second because the first is much more comfortable.
