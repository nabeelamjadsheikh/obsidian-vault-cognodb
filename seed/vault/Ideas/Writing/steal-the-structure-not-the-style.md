Everyone who tries to learn from a writer they love copies the wrong layer. You imitate the sentences — the cadence, the fondness for a certain kind of clause, the trick with the dash — and produce pastiche, which reads as costume and teaches almost nothing, because sentence style is downstream of a sensibility you do not have.

The transferable layer is architecture. How long does the piece wait before stating its claim? Where does the counterargument sit? How many examples, and are they front-loaded or dealt out? Where does it change altitude from the concrete to the general, and how many times? None of that is personal. It is engineering, and it works in your voice as well as theirs.

The exercise: take an essay you envy, write one line per paragraph describing its function, not its content. "Anecdote, no framing." "Names the pattern." "Concedes the strongest objection." You end up with a fifteen-line skeleton, which is a reusable object. Then write something entirely your own into it. The result rarely resembles the original, which is how you know you took the right thing.

This is the same operation as the skeleton read in [[The Shape of an Essay]], performed on someone else's work instead of your own. Used as a starting frame, a borrowed skeleton is the one case where a fairly detailed plan is safe — with the usual caveat from [[Outlines Are Scaffolding, Not Blueprints]] that it comes down once the draft stands.

It also fits the general case: [[Imitation Is Apprenticeship]] only works when the copying has a specific target. And because architecture is impersonal, borrowing it leaves your own [[Voice Is What Survives Translation]] intact, which is exactly what copying sentences destroys.
