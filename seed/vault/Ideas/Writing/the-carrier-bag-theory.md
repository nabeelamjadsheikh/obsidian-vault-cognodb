Le Guin's essay makes an argument about prehistory in order to make one about form. The first human tool was probably not the spear but the container — the net, the sling, the bag that let you carry back more oats than you could eat standing up. But the story we tell about ourselves is the hunting story: conflict, thrust, kill, triumph. A story shaped like a weapon.

She proposes the alternative: fiction as a carrier bag. Not a hero moving through a plot toward a climax, but a container that gathers things which are interesting together and lets them sit in relation. The novel as a medicine bundle, holding things in a particular, powerful relation to one another and to us. I put the wider argument about what stories are for in Le Guin on What Stories Are For.

I did not expect this to change how I write non-fiction, but it did. Most advice about essays assumes a spear: thesis, evidence, opposition defeated. Some subjects genuinely have that shape. Many do not — they are collections of observations that belong together, and forcing them into a conflict shape amputates the parts that do not fight. This is the real content of [[Narrative Versus Argument]]: the spear form is argument wearing a story costume.

The catch is that a bag still has a shape. A carrier bag essay is not formless; the container has a mouth, a bottom, and an order in which things come out. [[The Shape of an Essay]] applies just as much, only the structure is sequence and juxtaposition rather than escalation.
