King writes two thousand words a day, holidays included, and the number is less interesting than the structure around it: the door is closed, the quota is not negotiable, and he does not leave until it is met. The rest of what I took from the book is in [[Notes on On Writing]].

I have run this at 500 words for about two years now, and the effects were not the ones I expected.

**What I expected:** more output. True, but boring.

**What actually mattered:** the daily quota removes the decision. Without it, every session begins with a negotiation about whether today is a writing day, and that negotiation is where the willpower goes. With it, the only question is which words, not whether. This is the same mechanism that makes [[The Blank Page Problem]] tractable — the paralysis is a decision problem, and a quota deletes the decision.

**The unexpected part:** a quota makes bad writing cheap. When you owe 500 words, a bad 500 is a completed obligation, and you stop treating each session as a referendum on your talent. Over a year that is thousands of low-stakes attempts.

The failure mode is optimising the metric. Words are a proxy, and a proxy under pressure degrades — you will pad. My counterweight is that quota days and revision days are different days, and revision days are measured in cuts.

None of this addresses the harder problem. Producing words daily is not the same as producing finished things, and [[Finishing Is a Separate Skill]] with its own practice.
