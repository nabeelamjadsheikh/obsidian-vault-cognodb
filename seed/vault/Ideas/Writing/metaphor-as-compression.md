Hofstadter's claim — that analogy is not a rhetorical ornament but the core of cognition — reframes what a metaphor is doing in a sentence. It is not making the prose prettier. It is shipping a whole inference structure in six words: relations, constraints, expected behaviours, all transferred at once from a domain the reader already owns.

Say "the bottleneck is the review queue" and the reader instantly imports flow, capacity, backpressure, the pointlessness of speeding up upstream stages. You could have written four paragraphs. You wrote five words, and they arrived as a unit.

Which is exactly why bad metaphors are so dangerous. They also arrive as a unit, and the reader cannot easily unpack which parts they were meant to accept. "The brain is a computer" smuggles in addressable storage, a clean hardware-software split, and lossless retrieval, none of which survive contact with evidence. The reader inherits all of it silently.

Two working rules:

- Before using one, list three inferences it licenses and check whether you endorse all three. If one is wrong, the metaphor is a liability, not a shortcut.
- Beware the metaphor that is doing the arguing. If removing it collapses your case, you had an image, not a claim.

For teaching, this is the best available escape from [[The Curse of Knowledge]], since it lets you hand over structure rather than vocabulary — the practical form of which is [[Explain It To a Twelve-Year-Old]]. And because which images a writer reaches for is remarkably stable, metaphor is a large fraction of what I mean in [[Voice Is What Survives Translation]].
