Readers feel structure the way they feel the floor: only when it is missing. A piece that sags in the middle produces a vague reluctance to continue, and the reader almost never diagnoses it as structure. They say it was boring, or too long, and both are symptoms.

The shapes I actually use, and what each is for:

- **The claim and its complications.** State the position early, spend the body attacking it honestly, arrive somewhere more qualified. Good when you have a real position and the reader has objections.
- **The observation ladder.** Small concrete thing, slightly larger pattern, the general principle, back down to a second concrete thing. Good when the general claim would sound absurd stated cold.
- **The gathering.** No escalation, just related things placed carefully in sequence. This is the form [[The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction]] argues for, and it is much harder than it looks because the only thing holding it together is juxtaposition.
- **The reversal.** The obvious answer, why it is nearly right, the thing it misses.

Which shape you can use is decided before you draft, by the choice described in [[Narrative Versus Argument]] — the escalating shapes want argument, the gathering wants narrative.

Structure is discovered more often than designed, which is why [[Outlines Are Scaffolding, Not Blueprints]]: the outline gets you to a draft, then the draft tells you its real shape and you rebuild.

The fastest way to learn shapes is to reverse-engineer essays you admire, one line per paragraph, until the skeleton is visible. That is the practice described in [[Steal the Structure, Not the Style]], and it transfers in a way that imitating sentences never does.
