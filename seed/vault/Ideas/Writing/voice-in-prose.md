People talk about voice as though it were personality leaking through the prose — the writer being charming on the page. That model is wrong and produces insufferable writing, because it makes voice into performance.

Le Guin's model is better and more mechanical. Voice is a set of stable choices, made so consistently that they stop looking like choices: how long the sentences run before they turn, whether the verbs do the work or the adjectives do, what gets named specifically and what gets waved at, where the writer's attention goes when there is a choice about where to look. That is why [[Sentence Rhythm Carries Meaning]] is not a separate topic from voice; it is most of voice.

The test is translation. Take a paragraph into another language and the wordplay evaporates, but the attention pattern survives — you can still tell a Le Guin paragraph from a Hemingway one in Japanese. What survives is the voice. What died was the style.

I keep the rest of the exercises in Notes on Steering the Craft. The most useful of them is the one where you write the same scene twice at different sentence lengths, which teaches more in an hour than a year of being told to find yourself.

The other engine of voice is the analogy habit. Which images a writer reaches for is a real signature, and [[Metaphor as Compression]] explains why: choosing a metaphor is choosing an entire inference structure, so a writer's metaphors reveal how they think. And the ear is the only reliable instrument here, which is why [[Read It Aloud]] is the first edit and not the last.
