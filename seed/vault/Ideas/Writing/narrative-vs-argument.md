An argument tries to compel: here are premises you accept, here is a conclusion you now cannot escape. A narrative tries to earn: here is a sequence of things that happened, and by the end you find you have arrived somewhere.

Arguments have a failure mode. A reader who feels compelled resists, and resistance is cheap — they only need to doubt one premise. Narratives have the opposite failure mode: they can walk you somewhere you would never have agreed to go, without ever exposing a claim you could check. One is easy to refuse, the other is hard to audit.

Almost all good non-fiction smuggles one inside the other. The essay that changed your mind probably told you a story and let you do the inference yourself, or made an argument whose examples were small narratives you could not dismiss. This is what [[The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction]] is proposing as a formal option — that gathering can do the work we assume only conflict can.

The choice of form is also a choice about which shape the piece will need, so it precedes any decision made in [[The Shape of an Essay]]. Get this wrong and no amount of structural repair helps.

The compression device that sits between the two is the figure of speech: a metaphor is a tiny narrative doing an argument's job, which is why [[Metaphor as Compression]] deserves suspicion as well as admiration.

And the reason the choice matters at all is that form determines what you can think. [[Writing Is Thinking, Not Its Transcript]] means picking argument commits you to ordering claims; picking narrative commits you to ordering time. You will discover different things.
