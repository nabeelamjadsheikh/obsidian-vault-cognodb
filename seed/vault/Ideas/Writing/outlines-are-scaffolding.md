title: Outlines Are Scaffolding, Not Blueprints

Two failure modes, symmetric. Without an outline I write four unrelated good paragraphs and abandon the piece. With a detailed outline I write the piece I planned, which is always duller than the piece I would have discovered, because the plan was made by the version of me who had not yet thought it through.

The resolution is in the metaphor. Scaffolding holds the building up while it is being built and is then removed; nobody confuses it with the structure. Blueprints specify the finished thing in advance and are binding. Outlines should be the former and are usually treated as the latter.

Alexander is useful here, though he was writing about buildings. His whole objection to the modern design process is that the plan is fixed before the thing is alive, so the life gets designed out. Unfolding is his word — the structure emerges through a sequence of local decisions, each responding to what the previous one made possible. Prose behaves the same way. You cannot know paragraph six until paragraph five exists, and an outline that pretends otherwise is lying about the order of knowledge.

So: outline coarsely, four or five beats, no sub-bullets, and expect to throw it away. Its actual function is to make starting possible, which is the only thing that reliably solves [[The Blank Page Problem]]. Once the draft can stand on its own, take the scaffolding down and find out what shape the thing really has, using the skeleton read described in [[The Shape of an Essay]].

The exception: if you are working in a borrowed structure — the practice in [[Steal the Structure, Not the Style]] — a tighter outline is fine, because the structure has already been proved by someone else's finished piece.
