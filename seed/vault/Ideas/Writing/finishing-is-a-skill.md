I have started perhaps four hundred pieces and finished maybe seventy. For a long time I read that ratio as a taste problem — most ideas do not deserve completion — which was flattering and mostly false. The real ratio problem is that starting and finishing are different skills, and only one of them was getting practice.

Starting is intoxicating. The idea is at its best before contact with the page, the piece is still perfect because it does not exist, and the first thousand words come fast. Finishing is a completely different job: the last twenty percent is negotiation with disappointment, because the thing on the page is now definitely worse than the thing in your head, and the only way through is to accept the worse version.

King's version of this is that stopping is the actual danger, and momentum is a resource you spend rather than find. A steady rate makes any single day less decisive, which is the practical function of [[The Daily Quota]].

What made a difference for me:

- **Deciding in advance what "done" means.** Not "good", but a specific condition: the argument is stated, the two obvious objections are answered, it is under 1200 words.
- **Treating the end-stage cuts as routine.** [[Kill Your Darlings, Gently]] is easier when it is scheduled work rather than a crisis of confidence.
- **Finishing small things deliberately, for practice.** Finishing compounds; the more pieces you have ended, the less the ending feels like a verdict. This is the same argument as Quantity Produces Quality, applied to completions rather than attempts.

And the entry cost falls too. A page you have finished a hundred pieces on is a much less frightening object, which quietly deflates [[The Blank Page Problem]].
