Le Guin opens Steering the Craft by insisting that prose is sound, that the ear is the writer's instrument, and that the whole book should be read out loud. I ignored this for years because it seemed like a mystical claim about the music of language. It is not. It is a claim about error detection rates.

The eye is a forgiving reader. It skips, it predicts, it repairs. Having written the sentence, you have the intended version in your head, and the eye happily supplies it over whatever is actually on the page. The mouth cannot do that. It has to produce every word, in order, at speed, with breath.

What reading aloud catches in one pass, ranked by how often it catches it:

- Sentences with no place to breathe. Always a symptom, sometimes of a buried clause, sometimes of a buried idea.
- Accidental repetition — the same word three times in a paragraph, invisible on screen, glaring in the mouth.
- False emphasis, where the stress lands on a word that is not the point. This is where the rhythm work in [[Sentence Rhythm Carries Meaning]] stops being theory.
- Sentences the writer is enjoying too much. Darlings are audible, which makes this the best detector for [[Kill Your Darlings, Gently]].

It also stress-tests voice, since [[Voice Is What Survives Translation]] is largely a sound signature — if the paragraph does not sound like you saying it, something borrowed has crept in.

One warning: this is a late-stage tool. Reading aloud during composition strangles the draft, and it will not tell you the piece is about the wrong thing. That is the job of [[Revision Is Re-Seeing]], and no amount of good sentences substitutes for it.
