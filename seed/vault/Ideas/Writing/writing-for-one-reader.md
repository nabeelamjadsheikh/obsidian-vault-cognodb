Every difficult decision about a piece — how much to explain, how formal to be, how long to run, whether that joke lands — dissolves the moment you name a specific person you are writing to. Not a demographic. A person, with a name, whose face you can picture reading it.

The reason this works is that "the reader" is an average, and averages have no preferences. Asked whether to define a term for the general reader you will dither forever. Asked whether Andrew already knows what a graph traversal is, you answer in half a second and move on.

It is also the cheapest available defence against [[The Curse of Knowledge]]. You cannot simulate not knowing things, but you can simulate one particular person not knowing them, because you have watched them be confused before.

Two practical notes:

- Pick someone slightly less expert than your median actual reader. Overshooting on clarity costs an expert five seconds of impatience; undershooting costs a novice the whole piece. That asymmetry is the practical core of [[Plain Language Is a Form of Respect]].
- Write the opening as though continuing a conversation with them, then delete the throat-clearing. What remains is usually a real opening, and it will honour [[The First Sentence Is a Contract]] by accident, because you were never going to lie to someone you know.

The failure mode is writing to impress your imagined reader rather than to be understood by them. If the person you picked is someone you want to look clever in front of, pick someone else.
