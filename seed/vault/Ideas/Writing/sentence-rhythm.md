A long sentence that keeps deferring its verb, accumulating clauses, holding you in suspension while the subject waits somewhere behind the commas, makes you feel one way. Then a short one lands. That is not decoration. The reader's body has already received an instruction about how to feel before the semantic content arrives.

Le Guin's chapter on this is blunt: if you cannot hear your prose you cannot write it. She has an exercise where you write a paragraph in sentences of no more than seven words, then rewrite it as one unbroken sentence of three hundred. Both are unpleasant. Doing both makes you permanently aware of what length is doing, which is the point, and it is the fastest route to what I described in [[Voice Is What Survives Translation]].

Three things I now watch for:

- **Monotony of length.** Four consecutive twenty-word sentences read as bureaucratic even when each is fine. The problem is the pattern, not the sentence.
- **Stress at the end.** English puts weight on the last word of a clause. Wasting that slot on a preposition or a hedge throws away free emphasis.
- **Where the reader breathes.** [[Read It Aloud]] finds these instantly; the eye skips them every time.

Rhythm also does moral work. Short, unadorned, concrete sentences are the ones people trust, which is half the argument in [[Plain Language Is a Form of Respect]]. And the opening cadence is the strongest signal a reader gets about what kind of piece this is, which is why [[The First Sentence Is a Contract]] is about sound as much as about content.
