An opening line does more work than any other sentence, and almost none of it is informational. It tells the reader what kind of thing this is, how much attention it will demand, what register it will keep, and roughly how it will end. The reader signs, mostly unconsciously, and then holds you to it.

Breaking the contract is punished silently. Nobody writes to say your third paragraph betrayed the tone your first one promised. They just stop, and they attribute the stopping to being busy.

The common breaches:

- **Register drift.** A wry, conversational opener followed by four paragraphs of institutional prose. The reader feels bait-and-switched even if the institutional prose is good.
- **Scale mismatch.** Opening on a huge claim and delivering a narrow observation. The narrow observation may be the more valuable thing, and it is now a disappointment.
- **Form mismatch.** Promising a story and delivering a taxonomy. This is the choice made in [[Narrative Versus Argument]], and the first sentence has already announced it whether you meant it to or not.

The practical consequence is that the opening should be written last, or at least rewritten last, once you know what you have actually delivered — the structural read from [[The Shape of an Essay]] tells you what the promise ought to be.

Two more things it sets. The cadence of the first sentence establishes the sound of the piece, which is why [[Voice Is What Survives Translation]] is at stake in twelve words. And the pitch of the address — how much you assume, how formal you are — is answered instantly if you have done [[Write For One Reader]], because you are simply saying the first thing you would say to them.
