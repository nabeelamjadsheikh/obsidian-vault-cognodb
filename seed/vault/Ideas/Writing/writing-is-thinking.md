The folk model of writing is dictation: you have a thought, and then you type it out. If that were true, thinking would be the hard part and writing would be typing. Everyone who has actually written something knows the reverse. You sit down with what feels like a complete idea, write the first paragraph, and discover the idea had three unexamined joints in it.

The sentence is a test rig. It forces sequence onto something that was pleasantly simultaneous in your head, and sequence is where the contradictions surface. This is exactly why [[The Blank Page Problem]] is so painful — the page is not waiting for your thought, it is about to audit it.

It also explains why [[Revision Is Re-Seeing]] is not cosmetic work. If the first draft is where the thinking happened, the second draft is where you find out what you thought.

## Consequences

- Never wait until you understand something to write about it. Writing about it is the mechanism by which you come to understand it.
- The difference between [[Narrative Versus Argument]] is partly a difference in which kind of thinking the form makes you do: one makes you order events, the other makes you order claims.
- Keep the thinking-writing and the presenting-writing apart, which is the whole case in [[Notebooks Are Not Drafts]].

The practical upshot is unglamorous. If you are stuck on a problem, stop staring at it and write a bad paragraph about it.
