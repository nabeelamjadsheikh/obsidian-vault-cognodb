Once you know something, you cannot reconstruct what it was like not to know it. The knowledge is not a fact you hold at arm's length; it has rewired what looks obvious, what needs saying, and where the joints of the subject are. Ignorance is not a state you can visit again by trying.

This is the whole explanation for why expert prose is so bad. It is not arrogance, mostly. It is that the expert genuinely cannot see the missing step, because for them there is no step there — two ideas that a beginner experiences as separate have fused into one chunk. The gap is invisible from above.

Because you cannot introspect your way out, the fixes are all external:

- Make a real person the audience, as in [[Write For One Reader]], and use their actual past confusions instead of your imagined ones.
- Force the explanation down to a level where no fused chunk can hide, which is the mechanism behind [[Explain It To a Twelve-Year-Old]].
- Watch someone use your explanation without helping them. Painful, and the only ground truth available.

There is a darker version. Some of the impenetrability is not a bug: [[Jargon as Gatekeeping]] describes the case where the difficulty is doing social work, sorting readers into insiders and outsiders. The two are hard to tell apart from the outside, and often from the inside too.

The escape hatch that works best is analogy — [[Metaphor as Compression]] — because a good metaphor hands the reader a structure they already own instead of asking them to build yours from parts.
