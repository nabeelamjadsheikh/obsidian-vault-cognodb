> Almost all good writing begins with terrible first efforts. You need to start somewhere.

Anne Lamott's chapter is the most useful thing ever written about the mechanics of getting words out, and the reason is that it is not encouragement. It is a description of process. Everyone whose finished prose you admire produced a first version that would embarrass them, and they did not experience that as failure, because they knew it was stage one of three.

What I keep relearning is that permission has to be granted deliberately and out loud. The moment I start editing the sentence I am currently writing, the draft dies. Editing during composition is the single most expensive habit I have. I collected the rest of what stuck from the book in Notes on Bird by Bird.

The pairing that makes this work: the bad draft is only safe because [[Revision Is Re-Seeing]] is a real, scheduled, separate activity. If you never revise, permission to write badly just produces bad writing. If you do revise, the bad draft is raw material and its badness is irrelevant.

It also depends on volume. A single terrible draft is demoralising; the fourth one this month is just Tuesday, which is why [[The Daily Quota]] matters more than any technique.

And it needs a matching ruthlessness later — [[Kill Your Darlings, Gently]] is the tax you pay for having written freely.
