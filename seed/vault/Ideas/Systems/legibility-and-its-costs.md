Scott's argument, compressed: a state cannot administer what it cannot read, so it reshapes what it governs into a form that can be counted from the capital. Surnames, cadastral maps, standard weights, grid streets, scientific forestry. Each is a translation of a messy local reality into a schema.

The translation is not free, and the costs are systematic rather than accidental. The classic case is German scientific forestry — rows of a single species, planted and harvested on a schedule, magnificently measurable. The first rotation produced record yields. The second collapsed, because the forest had been an ecosystem of undergrowth, fungi, insects and soil chemistry, none of which was in the schema, all of which was doing necessary work. They had to invent a word for the failure: Waldsterben.

The general shape: the schema keeps what can be counted and discards local knowledge that was load-bearing but unmeasured. Then interventions are optimised against the schema and destroy the substrate.

This is why I distrust most measurement programmes without opposing them. The counting is not neutral observation; it is an intervention that changes what exists, and once a schema is in place everything that follows is optimisation against it — which is [[Goodhart's Law]] with an extra step, and the reason it is so hard to escape.

Three connected notes. Making something countable is a boundary decision — [[System Boundaries Are Choices]] — and everything outside the schema becomes an externality by construction. The local knowledge being destroyed is precisely the informal, unwritten adaptation that makes [[Cities as Emergent Systems]] work, which is why planned neighbourhoods feel dead in a way nobody can quite point at.

And the deep reason we do it anyway is that no central authority can hold the real thing in mind. Schemas are what [[Bounded Rationality]] does at scale: not stupidity, but the price of governing something larger than any model of it.
