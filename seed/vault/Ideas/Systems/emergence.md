Emergence is what we call a regularity at one level that has no counterpart at the level below. Temperature is emergent: no molecule has a temperature. Traffic jams are emergent: no car is jammed. A market price is emergent, and so is a flock's shape.

The word attracts mysticism, which is a shame, because the deflationary reading is more interesting. Nothing extra is added at the higher level — no new substance, no vital force. What appears is a *description* that is enormously cheaper and more predictive than the one below it. You can predict the jam without simulating the cars, and the prediction is good.

That is the epistemic version: emergence is about the level at which regularity becomes describable. It also means the higher description is not a convenience but a discovery — some patterns are only visible from there, which is the case made in [[More Is Different]].

Two things I find consistently true of emergent structures:

- **They are made of local rules and no global plan.** Boids need three rules, no leader. And the thing everyone wants to do with emergent order — design it directly, from above — is the one thing that reliably destroys it, which is what I keep circling in [[Cities as Emergent Systems]].
- **They require a stable substrate.** Levels come from parts that hold together internally and interact weakly across, which is Simon's argument in [[Hierarchy and Near-Decomposability]] — without near-decomposability there are no levels for anything to emerge at.

The design question is what you can do if you cannot specify the outcome. Alexander's answer is that you specify local resolutions and let the whole assemble itself, which is what [[Pattern Languages]] are for: rules at the level where people actually act, generating coherence nobody drew.
