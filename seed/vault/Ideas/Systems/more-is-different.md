Anderson's argument against naive reductionism is not that reductionism is false. He grants it entirely: everything is made of the things below it, with no exceptions and no extra ingredients. His claim is that the constructionist converse fails — being able to reduce everything to simple laws does not imply the ability to start from those laws and reconstruct the universe.

Why not: at each level of scale and complexity, entirely new properties appear, and understanding them requires research as fundamental as any other. Symmetry breaking is his central case. The laws are symmetric; the ammonia molecule is not; a crystal is not. Something happens at scale that is not present in the equations you started with.

The practical consequence, which I take into non-physics contexts constantly, is that "it's just X at bottom" is almost always a way of refusing to do the harder work at the level where the question actually lives. Psychology is not just chemistry in any useful sense. An organisation's behaviour is not just its individuals' behaviour, even though it is composed of nothing else.

This is the strong-form justification for [[Emergence]] as a real category rather than a description of our ignorance. New laws at each level, not merely new vocabulary.

It also explains why levels exist at all in the first place — you get stable intermediate structures because complexity assembles from nearly independent subsystems, which is the argument in [[Hierarchy and Near-Decomposability]].

And it is why the standard intervention on a large human system fails: [[Cities as Emergent Systems]] behave according to laws at the city level that are not recoverable from anybody's intentions, and planners who reason from the individual level get the level wrong, not the details.
