Efficiency at the wrong objective is worse than inefficiency, because it arrives sooner. The incompetent organisation pointed in a bad direction wanders and something interrupts it. The competent one gets all the way there.

This is the least discussed danger of getting good at things. Every improvement in execution is an amplifier on the objective, and the objective is the part nobody checks, because objectives get set once in an environment that then changes.

How it happens, in the order I usually see it:

1. A real goal exists but is not measurable, so a proxy is adopted with everyone's eyes open.
2. The proxy is easier to reason about, so planning attaches to it.
3. Two years pass. The people who remember the original goal have moved on, and the proxy is now the goal in every document. This is [[Goodhart's Law]] completing its full cycle — not gaming, just institutional memory loss.
4. Effort is now applied to the proxy with total sincerity and increasing skill.

The defences are all about the goal rather than the effort. Meadows puts goals near the very top of the intervention ladder in [[Leverage Points]] precisely because a system's purpose dominates its parameters, and she notes that the true purpose is revealed by behaviour, not by the mission statement.

Two structural cousins. Deciding what counts as success depends on where you drew the edge, and things outside the boundary are invisible to any optimiser — [[System Boundaries Are Choices]]. And the largest historical instances of this failure are the ones where a shared resource was optimised per-actor with the aggregate signal too weak to register, which is [[The Tragedy of the Commons]].

A cheap counterweight: whenever you make something more efficient, write down what would have to be true for this to be worth doing at all — then check it annually. Legibility makes optimisation easy and often destroys what was working, per [[Legibility and Its Costs]].
