Nobody designed the rhythm of a good neighbourhood. Jacobs' account of the Hudson Street sidewalk ballet is a description of an order with no author: the longshoremen at dawn, the school run, the lunch trade, the kids after three, the bar crowd at eleven, and a continuous, unplanned supply of eyes on the street that no police force could have provided.

What produces it is a pile of local conditions, none of which mentions safety or vitality: short blocks so routes multiply, mixed primary uses so different people arrive at different hours, buildings of varying age so rents vary and marginal businesses survive, and enough density to support all of it. Change any one and the ballet stops.

The reliable finding is that this order cannot be produced by specifying it. Every attempt to design the *outcome* directly — the plaza where people will gather, the mixed-use development with the correct proportions — produces a place with the shape of vitality and none of it. You can build the conditions or you can build the appearance, and the appearance does not generate the conditions.

Three notes on why:

- The order lives at a level that has no counterpart in any individual's intentions, which is the case in [[Emergence]], and it obeys regularities of its own — the point in [[More Is Different]].
- The planner's model necessarily discards the informal, uncounted arrangements that were doing the work: the shopkeeper who holds keys, the corner where deliveries happen. That destruction is exactly the process in [[Legibility and Its Costs]], and it is why the renewal projects that measured everything failed hardest.
- The only design method I know that engages honestly with this is Alexander's: specify local resolutions at the scale where people actually build, and let coherence assemble. [[Pattern Languages]] is a serious attempt to generate an outcome you are not allowed to draw.
