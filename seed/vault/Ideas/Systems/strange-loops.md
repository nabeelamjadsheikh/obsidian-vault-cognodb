A strange loop is what you get when moving through a hierarchy in a consistent direction returns you to where you started. Escher's staircase. The hands drawing each other. A canon that modulates upward through the circle of keys and arrives home an octave lower than it should.

Gödel's construction is the mathematical instance and the one that carries the weight. A formal system rich enough to describe arithmetic can encode statements *about* its own statements as numbers — so a level that was supposed to sit above the system gets pushed inside it. Then you can write a sentence that says it is not provable in that system, and the hierarchy has folded.

Hofstadter's leap, which is either the great insight of the book or an enormous metaphor depending on the day: a self is the same kind of object. A brain models the world, the model gets rich enough to include the brain modelling, and the loop closes. Nothing was added — no homunculus, no soul-stuff — but a symbol appeared that refers to the whole system in which it occurs, and that symbol is what we mean by "I". My longer working notes are in Notes on Gödel, Escher, Bach.

I find this most useful as a general warning about level-crossing. Layered architectures assume the layers stay apart. When a system can represent itself, they do not, and the resulting behaviour is not derivable from the layer diagram — which is why [[Self-Reference in Systems]] shows up wherever anything models its own operation.

Read this way, a strange loop is [[Emergence]] with the levels tangled: the higher-level phenomenon is real, and it is made of nothing but the lower level referring to itself. It is also what happens to Simon's clean picture in [[Hierarchy and Near-Decomposability]] when a subsystem takes the whole hierarchy as its input.
