A loop exists when a stock's level influences a flow that changes that same stock. That is all. It is the smallest structure capable of explaining behaviour that a list of parts cannot, and it is the actual unit of systems thinking.

Why the parts list fails: parts explain what a system is made of, and behaviour over time comes from how the parts are wired to each other. Two organisations with identical people and identical budgets produce opposite behaviour because the information flowing back is different. This is the reason blaming individuals rarely fixes anything — swap them out and the loop reproduces the behaviour with new personnel.

The discipline of drawing one properly, which I keep having to relearn:

- Name the stock first, precisely. Not "morale" but "number of people willing to raise a problem in a meeting."
- Then ask what information about the stock reaches whoever controls the flows, and how accurately.
- Then ask how long that information takes to arrive. Almost every pathology I have diagnosed lived in this third question, and the consequences are in [[Delays Cause Oscillation]].

There are only two kinds, and the whole taxonomy of system behaviour comes from combining them, which is the subject of [[Balancing and Reinforcing Loops]].

The prerequisite is being clear about what you are measuring, because a loop is defined in terms of a stock and a rate — hence [[Stocks and Flows]] comes first, and confusing the two produces diagrams that look right and predict nothing.
