Meadows' list runs from twelve up to one, weakest to strongest, and its real value is the observation that people intervene at the weak end almost exclusively — and that they often push in the wrong direction at the strong end.

The weak end: constants, parameters, subsidies, tax rates, standards. This is where nearly all political argument and nearly all corporate strategy happens. Changing a number rarely changes behaviour, because the number was not producing the behaviour; the structure was.

The middle: buffer sizes, physical structure, delay lengths, the strength of balancing and reinforcing loops. Real leverage, mostly ignored because it is slow and unglamorous. Shortening a delay is one of the most powerful things you can do to a misbehaving system and is almost never proposed — a point that follows directly from [[Feedback Loops]] and the way information timing dominates behaviour.

The strong end: information flows (who knows what, and when), rules, self-organisation, goals, and finally paradigms. Adding a feedback link that did not exist — showing a household its own electricity use in real time — is close to free and outperforms most price interventions.

Two cautions I have earned.

Meadows' own warning is that the highest leverage points are the ones where people most reliably push backwards, because the direction is counterintuitive. And the second: pulling a lever hard on the wrong objective just gets you to the wrong place faster, which is the whole content of [[Optimising the Wrong Thing]].

Note also that the goal-level leverage point is where measurement lives, so a system whose goal has been replaced by its metric is being levered at the strong end by accident.

Two practical prerequisites: know which quantity is a level and which is a rate, per [[Stocks and Flows]], and be explicit that you have chosen where the system ends, since [[System Boundaries Are Choices]] determines which levers you can even see.
