The story: a shared pasture, each herder adds a cow, each gets the full benefit of their own cow and one-nth of the cost of the overgrazing. Everyone rationally adds cows, the pasture collapses, everyone loses.

Meadows' framing is the useful one and it is not about greed. It is an **information** failure. The individual actor receives a strong, immediate, personal signal about their own gain and a weak, delayed, diluted signal about the state of the shared stock. Behaviour follows the strong signal. Replace every herder with a saint and the structure still degrades the pasture, only more slowly and with more anguish.

That reframing tells you where the fixes live, and there are three:

1. **Educate and exhort.** Strengthen the weak signal by moral means. Historically the weakest, because it fights the loop structure rather than changing it.
2. **Privatise.** Redraw the boundary so the cost lands on the actor who caused it. Works when the resource is divisible, which fish and air are not — and this is exactly the operation described in [[System Boundaries Are Choices]], with all its arbitrariness.
3. **Regulate the commons.** Keep it shared, and add a real feedback link with teeth: quotas, monitoring, enforcement. Ostrom's field work shows communities doing this themselves, well, for centuries, which is the part the pessimistic version of the story leaves out.

Structurally it is a reinforcing loop per actor plus a balancing loop whose signal is too weak and too slow to reach them, which is the pairing described in [[Balancing and Reinforcing Loops]]. The general prescription — restore the missing feedback link — is the same one that solves half the pathologies in [[Feedback Loops]].

The failure mode of fix three is that monitoring requires the resource to be made countable, and counting a commons tends to flatten the local arrangements that were quietly working, which is the caution in [[Legibility and Its Costs]].
