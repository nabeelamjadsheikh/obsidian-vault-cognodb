Alexander opens The Timeless Way by insisting there is a central quality that living things and living places have, that it is objective and precise, and that it cannot be named. Then he spends a chapter circling it with words he explicitly rejects one after another: alive, whole, comfortable, free, exact, egoless, eternal. Each captures something and each is wrong.

I found this insufferable the first time. I now think it is the most honest move in the book, for two reasons.

First, any name would immediately become a target. Name it "elegance" and people optimise for elegance, produce the look of elegance, and the quality departs — which is [[Goodhart's Law]] applied to aesthetics, and it is exactly what happened to every design vocabulary that did get named.

Second, refusing the name forces the argument to be made by ostension. He shows a hundred courtyards and asks which ones people actually sit in. The quality is defined by what happens in the place, not by a property you could check on a drawing. That is a much stronger epistemic position than a definition would have been.

The unnamed quality is what the whole catalogue is aiming at: [[Pattern Languages]] are the operational half, the transmissible part, and the notes I keep on them are in Notes on A Pattern Language. The patterns exist because the quality cannot be specified directly, only generated.

I have tried to hold this line in my own work, and I wrote what came of it in Lantern — Design Principles — the honest version is that you know it by whether people return to the thing, not by whether it satisfies your criteria.
