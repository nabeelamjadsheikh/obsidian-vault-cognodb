A model earns its place when it predicts more than it costs to remember. That is the whole test, and it disqualifies most of what gets collected under "mental models" — a list of two hundred named biases and heuristics has a storage cost larger than its predictive yield, and in practice you retrieve none of them at the moment of decision.

Compression is the right frame because it makes the tradeoff explicit. Supply and demand is a few sentences long and generates correct expectations about a very large number of situations you have never seen. Enormous ratio. "Survivorship bias" is a phrase that fires reliably and changes what evidence you go looking for. Also good. Whereas most named effects fire only when you are already thinking carefully, which is when you did not need them.

Two implications I take seriously:

- **Prefer few models used constantly to many models used never.** The ones that pay are the ones that have become automatic, which means the count has to be small.
- **A model that explains everything after the fact compresses nothing.** If no observation could have surprised it, it stores no information about the world. This is the difference between a model and a vocabulary.

Cognitively a model is a chunk with reach: the same unitisation described in [[Chunking]], selected for predictive power rather than mere familiarity. And the reason to bother is that a compressed representation strips surface features, which is precisely the condition for [[Transfer Is the Whole Point]] — an uncompressed memory stays welded to its original context.

This also explains the shape of expertise. Experts hold a small number of extremely well-fitted models plus an enormous library of instances, which is the account in [[Expertise Is Mostly Pattern Recognition]].
