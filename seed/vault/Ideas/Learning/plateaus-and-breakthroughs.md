Skill curves are not smooth. They are long flats punctuated by short jumps, and almost everybody reads the flat as a verdict on their talent. It is nearly always a verdict on their method.

The typical shape: you learn a technique, it produces rapid improvement, and then it stops producing improvement because you have extracted what it had. Continuing to apply it faithfully — more hours, same drill — yields nothing, and the nothing is experienced as a ceiling. Ericsson's consistent finding is that the flats break when the practice design changes, not when effort increases.

So the diagnostic question at a plateau is not "am I working hard enough" but "what specifically is now the limiting factor, and is my current practice touching it at all?" Usually it is not. The drill that got you here was built for a weakness you no longer have.

Three things I check when I stall:

- **Is the target still just beyond me?** If the exercises are comfortable, they have stopped being [[Deliberate Practice]] and become performance, which maintains skill and does not build it.
- **Can I see the results in time to correct?** Long stalls very often turn out to be feedback problems in disguise — the argument in [[Feedback Latency Sets the Learning Rate]]. The signal went missing and I kept practising blind.
- **Am I working on new material or better representations of old material?** Late progress is mostly the second, since it is the pattern library that has to grow, per [[Expertise Is Mostly Pattern Recognition]].

The other honest possibility is that the plateau reflects a real limit at the effort you are willing to spend. That is a legitimate place to stop, and the story about the popular hours number in [[The Ten Thousand Hours Myth]] is largely about people misreading such stops as universal law.
