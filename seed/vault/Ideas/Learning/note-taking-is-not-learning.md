You can produce an immaculate set of notes with almost no comprehension. Transcription runs entirely on the source's structure; the words arrive in the right order because someone else put them in order, and your hand can follow while very little happens upstream of it.

Ahrens' insistence is that the learning happens at exactly one moment: when you are forced to restate the idea in your own words, without the source in front of you, in a form that will make sense to you in two years without its original context. Everything before that is logistics.

That constraint is doing several things at once. Rewriting is retrieval, so it gets the encoding benefit described in [[Retrieval Practice Beats Review]]. It exposes the parts you cannot restate, which are the parts you did not understand. And it destroys the comfortable ambiguity of a quotation, which can sit in a file for years looking like knowledge.

The corollary is that a system's value is not proportional to what it contains. A vault of ten thousand copied passages is a library with your name on it. A vault of four hundred reformulated claims is a mind you can query.

The unit matters too. A note that restates one claim can be connected, contradicted, and reused; a note that summarises a whole chapter can only be reread, which is the argument for [[Atomic Notes]].

This is why I gave up marking books entirely — see [[Why I Stopped Highlighting]] — and why I distrust any workflow whose main promise is that capture is now faster. Capture was never the bottleneck. Reformulation is, and it is supposed to be slow.
