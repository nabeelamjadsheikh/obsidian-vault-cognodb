Ericsson's actual claim is much narrower and much more useful than the version that escaped into business books. Practice improves performance only when it has a specific structure: a target just beyond current ability, full attention, immediate feedback, and repetition with correction. Everything else is experience, and experience alone produces plateaus, not expertise.

The classic demonstration is that a doctor with twenty years of practice is often no better than one with five. Twenty years of doing the job is not twenty years of practice, because the job supplies almost none of the four conditions — particularly the feedback, which arrives months later if at all.

That last condition is the one people skip, and it dominates. Skills where feedback is fast improve fast; skills where it is slow improve slowly regardless of effort, which is the whole argument in [[Feedback Latency Sets the Learning Rate]]. If you want to get better at something with slow feedback, most of your design effort should go into manufacturing a faster signal rather than into more hours.

What it feels like: not flow. Flow is performing at your current level, which is pleasant and not improving. Deliberate practice is uncomfortable and can only be sustained in short blocks, an hour or two, which is one of the findings the popular version dropped along with the rest in [[The Ten Thousand Hours Myth]].

When it stops working, the usual cause is that the method has been exhausted rather than the talent, which is the pattern in [[Plateaus and Breakthroughs]]. New level, new drill.

Mechanically what it builds is representations — the rich pattern library that makes experts see situations rather than compute them, which is the case made in [[Expertise Is Mostly Pattern Recognition]].
