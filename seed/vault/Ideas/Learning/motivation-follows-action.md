The folk model is motivation, then action. Wait until you feel like it, then do it. On this model a bad week is a motivation shortage and the fix is inspiration — a video, a book, a resolution.

The observed order is the reverse for anything sustained. Starting produces the state that makes continuing easy. Two minutes into the thing you did not want to do, you want to do it, and the wanting was manufactured by the doing.

Clear's version of this is mostly about lowering the activation cost until beginning requires no decision: shrink the first step to something absurd, make the cue automatic, remove every point at which a choice could occur. The two-minute rule is not about doing two minutes of work. It is about the fact that the two minutes generate the state in which twenty are available. My fuller notes on the book are in Notes on Atomic Habits.

The corollary that changed my practice: never trust a decision made at the start of a session. The reluctance you feel before starting is not information about the work, it is information about starting.

This is why a fixed daily obligation outperforms ambition, and why [[The Daily Quota]] survives bad weeks — the quota is met by beginning, and beginning is the only hard part.

It also reframes the discipline talk around skill acquisition. The unpleasantness of [[Deliberate Practice]] is real, but a large share of what people experience as insufficient motivation is really insufficient scaffolding around the first thirty seconds. Fix the entry cost and the motivation problem substantially dissolves.
