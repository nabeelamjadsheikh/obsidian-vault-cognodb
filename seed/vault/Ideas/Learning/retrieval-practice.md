Reading the chapter again feels like learning. Closing the book and writing what it said feels like failing. The feelings are exactly inverted relative to the effect.

Oakley's version is blunt: recall is the study technique, and almost everything else is decoration. Read a page, look away, reconstruct it. The reconstruction is where the encoding happens, because retrieving a memory changes it — it is not read-only. Every successful pull makes the next pull easier, and rereading does almost none of this while producing a much stronger sense of mastery.

That sense of mastery is the trap, and it deserves its own note: [[The Illusion of Competence]]. Fluency with material in front of you is not knowledge of material that is absent, and the only way to tell them apart is to remove the material.

The operational version is small and does not need software:

- After any chapter, close it and write three sentences on what it claimed.
- Before opening your notes on a topic, say out loud what you think is in them.
- Turn every heading in a text into a question you answer before reading the section.

Cheap ways to make this routine are collected in [[Using the Testing Effect Day to Day]].

Two amplifiers. Spacing the retrievals out multiplies the effect, which is the whole design of [[Spaced Repetition]]. And the strongest form of retrieval is explaining to someone else, because their confusion locates your gaps for you — see [[Learning by Teaching]], which is retrieval practice with an error signal attached.
