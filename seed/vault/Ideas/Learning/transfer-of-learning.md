Learning that does not appear in a new context is indistinguishable from memorising trivia. This sounds obvious and is routinely ignored, because every measurement we make of learning happens in the context where it was learned.

The depressing literature: transfer is much rarer than educators assume. Students who can solve a problem in physics class fail the identical problem posed as a chemistry problem. People trained on a puzzle solve a structurally identical puzzle with different surface details at chance rates until someone points at the analogy.

Hofstadter's diagnosis is that transfer is analogy, and analogy is the whole of cognition rather than a special trick. If two situations do not look alike to you, no mechanism will fire, and what makes them look alike is having a representation that ignores surface features. Which is another way of saying transfer requires the situation to be encoded at the right level of abstraction — the compression argument in [[Mental Models Are Compression]].

What appears to help:

- Learning the same structure in at least two superficially different domains. One instance and you have a story; two and you have a pattern.
- Naming the abstraction explicitly, so it can be retrieved on its own rather than only via its original context. Named abstractions are retrievable units, which is [[Chunking]] in service of transfer.
- Explaining it to someone in a different field, since they will strip your surface details for you — the useful violence of [[Learning by Teaching]].

The expert version of this is that skilled people recognise situations across domains automatically, which is the claim in [[Expertise Is Mostly Pattern Recognition]]. Transfer is not a bonus outcome of learning. It is the only outcome worth the name.
