The number comes from Ericsson's 1993 study of violinists at a Berlin conservatory. The best group had accumulated roughly ten thousand hours by age twenty. That is an average, in one domain, among people already selected for admission to an elite music school, measuring a very specific kind of solitary practice.

Every one of those qualifications was dropped in transmission, and Ericsson spent the rest of his career objecting to the result.

What got lost:

- **It was an average, not a threshold.** The spread was enormous. Some in the top group had far fewer hours, some in the middle group had more. A mean is not a requirement.
- **The hours were a particular kind of hours.** Solitary, effortful, targeted at specific deficiencies, with a teacher's correction — the conditions that define [[Deliberate Practice]]. Ordinary playing did not count, and the pop version counts everything.
- **The domain matters enormously.** Violin has centuries of pedagogy, stable rules and immediate feedback. In fields with none of that, hours predict far less, for reasons spelled out in [[Feedback Latency Sets the Learning Rate]].
- **Causality was never established.** Practice predicts performance and something also predicts who is willing and able to practise that way.

What survives, and it is not nothing: expert performance is overwhelmingly built rather than born, it takes a very long time, and the time is spent accumulating structured experience — the pattern library described in [[Expertise Is Mostly Pattern Recognition]].

The practical damage of the myth is that it tells people to count hours. Counting hours is exactly what you do when you have stopped changing your method, which is how [[Plateaus and Breakthroughs]] become permanent.
