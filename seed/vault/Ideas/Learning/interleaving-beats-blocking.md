Blocked practice is twenty problems of type A, then twenty of type B. Interleaved practice mixes them. Blocked practice produces better performance during the session and worse performance a week later, reliably, across motor skills, maths and category learning.

The explanation that convinced me: in a blocked set you only solve the problem once. After the first one you have loaded the method, and the remaining nineteen are execution. In a mixed set you have to identify which kind of problem this is before you can start — and identification is the skill that actually transfers, because real problems do not arrive labelled with the chapter they came from.

This is a specific case of the general result in [[Desirable Difficulties]]: the conditions that make practice feel worse often make learning better, and learners consistently choose the version that feels better.

Two practical notes.

**Do not interleave too early.** You need the method to exist before you can practise selecting it. Block until you can execute, then mix. The point of mixing is discrimination, not confusion.

**Interleaving and spacing are the same lever seen from two angles.** Mixing topics necessarily spaces each of them, and the scheduling logic in [[Spaced Repetition]] is doing the same job in the time dimension.

There is also a chunk-formation story. Discriminating between problem types is how you learn the boundaries of a chunk — see [[Chunking]] — and a chunk with fuzzy boundaries fires on the wrong situations.

The unpleasant part is that interleaved sessions feel bad. You get more wrong, progress feels absent, and the improvement only becomes visible after enough forgetting has happened for [[The Forgetting Curve]] to separate the two conditions.
