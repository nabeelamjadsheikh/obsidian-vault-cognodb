You read a worked solution, follow every step, feel the click, and conclude you can solve that problem. You cannot. You can follow that solution, which is a different capability that happens to feel identical from the inside.

Oakley names this precisely: fluency with material in front of you is mistaken for knowledge of material that is absent. Every stage of the reading felt effortless because the text was doing the retrieval for you, and effortlessness is the cue your brain uses to judge mastery.

The illusion is strongest exactly where it is most costly:

- Rereading notes before an exam. Maximum fluency, minimum learning.
- Watching a lecture at 1.5x while nodding. Comprehension is real; retention is not implied by it.
- Marking up a book, which produces a durable artifact of feeling and no capacity at all — see [[Why I Stopped Highlighting]].

The only reliable defence is removing the material and seeing what remains, which is why [[Retrieval Practice Beats Review]] is both a study method and a measuring instrument. It is the measuring function that matters most here: a failed recall attempt is information you cannot get any other way.

The related trap is thinking the artifact is the learning. A beautiful set of notes can be produced with almost no comprehension, which is the argument in [[Note-Taking Is Not Learning]] — the note only teaches you something at the moment you are forced to restate it without the source.

My rule after any input that felt easy: close it and write three sentences from memory. If they are thin, the ease was the illusion, and the material has not been learned at all.
