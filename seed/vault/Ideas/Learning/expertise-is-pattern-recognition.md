The naive model of expertise is that experts reason better — more steps, faster, fewer errors. The evidence says something stranger: experts reason *less*. They recognise the situation as one they have seen, and the recognition delivers the answer, or at least a very short list of candidate answers, before any deliberate reasoning starts.

Simon's estimate for chess mastery was on the order of fifty thousand stored patterns. The grandmaster looking at a position is not searching a tree; the two or three moves worth considering present themselves, and the search that follows is short and shallow. The same account fits fire commanders, radiologists, and experienced programmers reading a stack trace.

Three consequences.

**The pattern library takes years to build and cannot be shortcut.** Fifty thousand of anything is a decade. This is the true content behind the number in [[The Ten Thousand Hours Myth]], with the misleading parts removed.

**It is built by exposure with correction, not exposure alone.** Patterns that are never checked against outcomes calcify into confident nonsense, which is why [[Deliberate Practice]] insists on feedback as a defining condition rather than a nice-to-have.

**Recognition has a failure mode: it fires on surface similarity.** The expert sees a familiar shape and stops looking. Which is why abstraction matters — see [[Transfer Is the Whole Point]] — and why the very best practitioners keep a habit of asking what else this could be.

Mechanically the library is made of chunks, in exactly the sense described in [[Chunking]], and the stalls that punctuate skill growth are usually the library saturating on the current kind of input, which is the pattern in [[Plateaus and Breakthroughs]].
