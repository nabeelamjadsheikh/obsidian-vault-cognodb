For beginners, studying a fully worked solution beats attempting the problem, and the gap is large. This is one of the more robust findings in instructional research and it contradicts almost every piece of folk wisdom about productive struggle.

The mechanism is load. A novice attempting a problem spends nearly all available working memory on search — what do I even do first — and search leaves no capacity for noticing the structure of the solution. They may eventually get an answer, and learn very little from having got it, because the useful information was never attended to. A worked example removes the search and lets all the capacity go to the structure.

The critical qualification, which is why this does not license lecturing at people forever: the effect **reverses** with expertise. Once the learner can generate the solution, studying worked examples becomes redundant processing and actively wastes time. The same instruction that helps a novice hurts an expert.

That reversal is the reason this cannot be a fixed policy. Support has to be withdrawn on a schedule as competence grows, which is the argument in [[Scaffolding and Fading]]. The standard implementation is completion problems: a fully worked example, then one with the last step removed, then the last two, until nothing is given.

The relationship to solo effort is not opposition. Once the learner can frame the problem, targeted attempts with correction take over, which is the regime described in [[Deliberate Practice]]. The two are stages, not rivals.

Framed in terms of memory, a worked example is a pre-assembled chunk offered for study — see [[Chunking]] — and the reason it helps is that assembling it yourself, before you have the parts, is what was consuming the capacity.
