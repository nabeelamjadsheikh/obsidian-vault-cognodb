Working memory holds a small number of items, and that number is roughly fixed across people and stubbornly resistant to training. What is not fixed is what counts as one item.

The chess demonstration is still the cleanest. Masters reproduce a board after five seconds' exposure with near-perfect accuracy — but only when the position is from a real game. Randomise the pieces and they perform like beginners. They were never remembering twenty-five pieces. They were remembering four or five familiar structures, and the structures only exist in legal positions.

So expertise is not faster processing or bigger memory. It is a larger vocabulary of chunks, which lets a fixed-size working memory hold a much larger situation. This is the mechanism behind [[Expertise Is Mostly Pattern Recognition]], and Simon's hierarchical account of complex systems is the same idea one level up — see [[Hierarchy and Near-Decomposability]].

Two things this changes about how I learn.

**Chunks form through use, not exposure.** You do not chunk by reading about a pattern; you chunk by applying it until the parts stop being separate. And you learn a chunk's boundaries by having to discriminate it from neighbours, which is why [[Interleaving Beats Blocking]] builds better chunks than blocked drilling does.

**Deliberately compress.** After learning something, name it and state it in one line. A named thing is retrievable as a unit. This is the same operation as [[Mental Models Are Compression]] — a model is a chunk with predictive reach.

For teaching, the implication is that support should hold the not-yet-chunked parts and then be removed as chunks form, which is exactly the schedule in [[Scaffolding and Fading]].
