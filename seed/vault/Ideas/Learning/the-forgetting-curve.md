Ebbinghaus memorised nonsense syllables on himself for years and produced the first quantitative law of memory: retention falls off sharply at first and then slowly, roughly exponentially, and the shape is stable enough to plan around.

Two consequences follow, and they run in opposite emotional directions.

The bleak one: most of what you read is gone within days, and the loss is front-loaded. By the time you notice you have forgotten a book, you forgot the bulk of it in the first week, while you still felt like you knew it.

The useful one: because the decay is regular, it is schedulable. If forgetting were random you could do nothing but review constantly. Because it is a curve, you can intercept it just before the drop — which is the entire engineering basis of [[Spaced Repetition]]. Every review flattens the subsequent curve, so the intervals can expand.

The subtlety I under-rated for years is that the curve applies to a *particular* memory trace, and each successful retrieval creates a better one. So the curve is not a fact about the material; it is a fact about how the material was last encoded. A passive re-read barely resets it. An effortful recall resets it substantially.

Which is why difficulty pays: retrieving something at the point where you nearly cannot is what produces the flattest subsequent curve, the mechanism behind [[Desirable Difficulties]]. If you review too early it is easy, feels good, and buys you almost nothing.

You do not need software to exploit this. The low-friction habits in [[Using the Testing Effect Day to Day]] intercept the same curve informally. What does not work at all is marking a page and trusting your future self, for reasons in [[Why I Stopped Highlighting]].
