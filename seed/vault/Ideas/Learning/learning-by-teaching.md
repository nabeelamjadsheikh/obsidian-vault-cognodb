The reliable finding is that people who prepare to teach material learn it better than people who prepare to be tested on it — and the effect appears before any teaching happens. Expecting to explain changes how you read.

Why: reading for yourself lets you keep things in a fuzzy, half-connected state that feels like comprehension. Reading in order to explain forces you to decide what order the ideas go in, which one depends on which, and where the beginner will fall over. That reorganisation is real work and silent study never demands it.

Then the actual teaching adds an error signal you cannot generate alone. A learner's question is a probe aimed precisely at the joint you glossed. My experience is that the first question is almost always about the step I skipped, which I skipped because I did not understand it. That is not a coincidence; it is the whole value.

The solo approximation is writing the explanation and hunting for your own vagueness, which is [[The Feynman Technique]]. It catches a lot, but it cannot catch what you do not know to look for, so a real audience remains better.

For beginners on the receiving end, the counterintuitive result is that studying full solutions beats struggling — see [[The Worked Examples Effect]] — so a good explanation is not a lesser substitute for practice; early on it is the more efficient input.

This is also why a stack of tidy summaries is worth so little next to one explanation you had to deliver: the restatement is the learning, which is the argument in [[Note-Taking Is Not Learning]].

Two connected points. Preparing to teach is retrieval at the structural level, which is why it outperforms rereading in the way [[Retrieval Practice Beats Review]] predicts. And an explanation stripped of your original context is much likelier to transfer, which is the argument in [[Transfer Is the Whole Point]].
