Scaffolding is temporary support that lets a learner do something they could not yet do alone. Fading is removing it. The second word is the one that gets dropped, and dropping it converts teaching into dependence.

I see this most often in the tools I build rather than in classrooms. A wizard that walks someone through a task the first time is scaffolding. The same wizard, unchanged, on the two hundredth run is a cage — the user has never seen the underlying model and cannot do anything the wizard did not anticipate.

The design question is therefore not "how much support" but "on what schedule does it come off, and what signal triggers the removal". Good fading is contingent, not timed: the support goes when the learner demonstrates they no longer need it.

The clearest worked implementation is the completion sequence from [[The Worked Examples Effect]] — full solution, then the last step removed, then two, then the bare problem. Each step transfers one piece of the work back to the learner, and the schedule is driven by their success rate rather than by the calendar. This matters because the worked-example advantage reverses with expertise, so support that overstays actively harms.

The mechanism underneath is capacity. Scaffolding holds the parts that are not yet unitised so attention can go to the part being learned; as [[Chunking]] proceeds, those parts stop needing to be held, and the support becomes noise.

Once the learner can frame problems unaided, the regime should change entirely to targeted, feedback-rich attempts — [[Deliberate Practice]] — which is what real competence is built out of.

For the person teaching, the discipline is to keep asking what you are still doing that the learner could now do, which is easier to notice when you are explaining rather than doing, as in [[Learning by Teaching]].
