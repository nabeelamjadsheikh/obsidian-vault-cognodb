I do not run a card system for most things. The maintenance cost is real, and a decaying deck generates guilt that eventually poisons the whole practice. What I do instead is a set of habits that cost nothing and capture most of the effect described in [[Retrieval Practice Beats Review]].

**The three-sentence close.** After finishing anything substantial, close it and write three sentences on what it claimed. Not a summary of the structure — the claims. If I cannot produce three, I did not read it, I looked at it.

**Question the headings.** Before reading a section, turn its heading into a question and answer it from what I already know. Two seconds, and it makes the reading an act of confirmation and correction rather than absorption.

**The walk recall.** On the way somewhere, pick a topic and try to reconstruct it out loud. This is retrieval at maximum difficulty — no cues at all — and the failures are extremely informative.

**Say it before you look.** Before opening my notes on something, state what I think is in them. The delta between the guess and the reality is the only part worth rereading.

All four are just retrieval with the cues stripped, and they exploit the same decay curve the schedulers exploit — see [[The Forgetting Curve]] — without any scheduling. For the small set of things that genuinely need cold recall, I still use [[Spaced Repetition]], and keeping that set small is what keeps it alive.

The habit these replaced was marking up texts, which produced no retrieval at all, for the reasons in [[Why I Stopped Highlighting]].
