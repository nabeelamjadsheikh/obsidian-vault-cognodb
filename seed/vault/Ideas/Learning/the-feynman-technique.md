Four steps, and the third is the only one that matters.

1. Write the name of the thing at the top of a blank page.
2. Explain it as if to someone with no background, in your own words, without looking anything up.
3. **Notice where you go vague, hand-wavy, or reach for the technical term as a substitute for the explanation.** That is the boundary of your understanding, and it is invisible until you try to produce prose across it.
4. Go back to the source at exactly that point, and only that point.

The value is diagnostic. Most study time is spent uniformly across material you already know and material you do not, because you cannot tell them apart while reading. Step three is a localiser. It says: the gap is here, in the transition between these two paragraphs, where you wrote "and then it follows that".

It is a specific and rather demanding case of [[Retrieval Practice Beats Review]] — you are not just recalling facts but rebuilding the structure that connects them, which surfaces missing joints that flashcards never touch.

The reason it works so much better than reading is that reading is fluent and fluency lies. [[The Illusion of Competence]] is the failure mode this technique is designed to defeat: an explanation cannot be fluent over a gap, because you will hear yourself stall.

The strongest version has an actual audience, since a real listener asks the question you carefully avoided asking yourself — see [[Learning by Teaching]]. But the solo version costs a page and eleven minutes and I have never once run it without finding something.
