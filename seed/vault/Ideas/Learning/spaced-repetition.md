The finding is a century old and still under-exploited: reviewing material at expanding intervals produces far more retention per minute than reviewing it repeatedly in one sitting. Same total time, wildly different outcome. The interval is doing the work.

The mechanism is that retention is a function of difficulty at recall. Reviewing something you still remember perfectly teaches almost nothing; reviewing it at the edge of forgetting reconsolidates it much more strongly. Which means the scheduler is not an administrative convenience — it is the intervention. It exists to keep every review near the point of failure, and it can only do that because [[The Forgetting Curve]] is regular enough to predict.

Two things I got wrong for years.

**It is not a substitute for understanding.** Cards for things you do not understand are torture and do not stick. The pipeline is understand first, then schedule. Spacing preserves; it does not construct.

**The card must demand production.** A card you read is worth a fraction of a card you answer, because the value is in the pulling — the whole argument in [[Retrieval Practice Beats Review]]. My early decks were full of cloze deletions so easy they were effectively rereading, and they felt great and did nothing.

What I actually keep in a scheduled system now is small: definitions I need cold, numbers, names, the handful of formulas I use quarterly. Everything else goes through the lighter habits in [[Using the Testing Effect Day to Day]], which cost nothing to maintain.

The negative result that got me here: I abandoned marking up books entirely, for reasons in [[Why I Stopped Highlighting]]. Highlighting produced a beautiful record of what once felt important and zero retrieval.
