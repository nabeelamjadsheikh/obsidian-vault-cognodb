The core result, and one of the most useful facts I know: manipulations that slow down acquisition frequently improve long-term retention and transfer. Spacing, interleaving, testing instead of restudying, reduced feedback, even worse handwriting or a harder-to-read font, all show some version of this.

The consequence is uncomfortable. Learners judge methods by how learning feels during the session, and during the session the effective methods feel worse. So people's confidence in a study technique is roughly anti-correlated with its value. Left to introspection, everyone converges on rereading and highlighting.

Not all difficulty is desirable, and the distinction matters or this becomes an excuse for bad teaching. Difficulty is desirable when it forces the processing you want — retrieval, discrimination, reconstruction. It is just difficulty when it consumes attention on something irrelevant, like a badly organised text or an unfamiliar notation. The former is [[Interleaving Beats Blocking]]. The latter is a broken textbook.

The rule I use: does the difficulty make me do the target operation, or does it make me spend effort before I reach the target operation?

There is also a measurement reason to prefer the harder version. Easy study produces fluency, and fluency is systematically misread as mastery — the trap in [[The Illusion of Competence]] — so a method that is difficult is at least reporting your real state.

Practically this means designing your practice against your own preferences. Choose the harder retrieval over the easy review — [[Retrieval Practice Beats Review]] is the highest-yield instance. Let material get slightly cold before returning to it, since the value depends on the decay described in [[The Forgetting Curve]].

There is one difficulty that is not desirable at all, and that is grinding without pause. Hard problems need the alternation described in [[Diffuse and Focused Modes]] — the wandering phase is not slack, it is a different processing mode, and refusing to leave the desk removes it.
