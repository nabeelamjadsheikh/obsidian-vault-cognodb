Solemnity and seriousness are opposites often enough that I now treat solemnity as a warning sign. The people doing the most serious work are almost never the most solemn about it, and the correlation is strong enough that I use it to decide whose advice to take.

The mechanism, I think, is that solemnity is an attitude toward one's own standing, and seriousness is an attitude toward the work. Someone protecting their standing cannot try the ridiculous option, cannot be visibly wrong, cannot ask the naive question in the meeting. Someone serious about the work will do all three before lunch, because the work is the point and looking foolish is a rounding error.

Whimsy is not the absence of rigour. It is what rigour looks like when it is not being defended. Feynman's plate, Hofstadter's puns, Le Guin's genre — in each case a person doing genuinely hard work while entirely unwilling to be dignified about it.

Three connections:

- The permission to try the foolish option is precisely what the play frame supplies, which is the argument in [[Play Is Serious Work]].
- Curiosity is inherently undignified, since it follows the question wherever it goes rather than where a reputation would prefer, and that is half of why [[Curiosity as the Engine]] outlasts discipline.
- Whimsical constraints — arbitrary rules taken seriously — are among the most productive devices I know, per [[Constraints Breed Creativity]]. A rule is funny and binding at the same time.

There is a practical version of this. Solemn work is derivative work, because the safe move is the established one, and it forecloses the odd juxtapositions that [[Creativity Is Combinatorial]] depends on. And it is usually chasing the appearance of importance, which is the trap described in [[Originality Is Overrated]].
