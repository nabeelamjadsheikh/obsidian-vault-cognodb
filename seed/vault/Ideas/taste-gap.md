Ira Glass' formulation is the best description of the beginner's problem I know. You got into this because you have good taste. Your taste is the reason you can tell your own work is disappointing. For a long stretch — years, not months — your ability will not match your taste, and the gap is where nearly everybody quits.

The important part is that the gap is not evidence of a mistake. It is a structural consequence of taste developing faster than skill, because judging is cheap and doing is expensive. If your work satisfied you at year one, that would be the alarming outcome: it would mean your taste was as undeveloped as your hands.

What makes it survivable:

- **Knowing it is normal.** Almost all of the damage comes from misreading the gap as a verdict on talent rather than a phase everyone passes through.
- **Volume.** The gap closes by attempts, not by thinking about attempts, which is the pottery-class result in [[Quantity Produces Quality]].
- **Targeted copying.** Working deliberately in the shadow of someone better closes it fastest, because it gives your practice a specific goal instead of a vague ambition — see [[Imitation Is Apprenticeship]].
- **Practice aimed at the specific deficiency.** Generic hours do not close a specific gap; the conditions in [[Deliberate Practice]] do.

There is a second reason people stop, and it is not really about the gap. The work is bad, and producing bad work in view of your own taste is humiliating, and avoidance dressed as standards is very hard to distinguish from integrity — which is the case made in [[Creative Block Is Usually Fear]].

The uncomfortable good news: the gap never closes. It moves. Your taste keeps developing too, and the dissatisfaction is permanent equipment.
