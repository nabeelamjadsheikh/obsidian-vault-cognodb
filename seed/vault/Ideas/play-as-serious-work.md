Le Guin defended fantasy for decades against people who thought it was escapism, and her argument was never that it is fun. It was that imagination is a serious faculty, the one by which we consider what is not the case, and that a culture which treats it as childish has disabled its own capacity to think about alternatives.

The narrower version I keep for my own work: play is the only mode in which I will try the option that might make me look foolish. Under a serious frame, every attempt is a claim about my competence, so I only attempt things I expect to succeed at — which restricts me to what I have already done. Under a play frame the same attempt costs nothing, so the ridiculous option gets tried, and the ridiculous option is where the interesting results have all come from.

This is a real mechanism and not a mood. Play changes the accounting on failure, and everything about how far you will search follows from that accounting.

Three notes:

- Play needs rules to be play, which is why arbitrary formal constraints are so productive — the argument in [[Constraints Breed Creativity]]. Unbounded messing about is not play; it is drift.
- It needs unstructured, undirected time, which modern working conditions eliminate first. The signal that you have some is boredom, which is why [[Boredom Is a Signal]] rather than a fault.
- It is not the opposite of serious. The people producing the most serious work are usually the least solemn about it, which I have written about in [[Seriousness and Whimsy]].

The test I apply to my own projects: am I still willing to do something obviously stupid with this? When the answer becomes no, the project is already finished and I have not noticed.
