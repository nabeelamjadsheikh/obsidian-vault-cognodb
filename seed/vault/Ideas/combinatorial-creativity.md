Almost nothing is invented from nothing. What gets called a new idea is nearly always two existing things put in contact for the first time by someone who happened to be holding both.

If that is true, then the romantic model — waiting for inspiration to visit — is optimising the wrong variable entirely. Two things determine your output, and neither is inspiration:

**The input set.** You can only combine what you have. Someone who reads exclusively in one field has a large number of elements that all combine in known ways, which is why the interesting recombinations so often come from people with an odd second interest. Range is not dilettantism; it is inventory.

**The collision rate.** Elements have to actually meet. Ideas filed in separate folders, read once and never revisited, never touch. This is the entire case for a linked vault over a hierarchy, and the practical reason I keep half-formed things together to ferment — see [[The Idea Compost Heap]].

There is a hard constraint on which combinations are even reachable at any moment, and that is the argument in [[The Adjacent Possible]] — it explains why the same idea arrives to four people in one year and to nobody in the previous century.

Two consequences I act on. Collecting has to be selective or the inventory becomes noise, which is why [[Collecting With Intent]] insists on a question first. And the mystique around novelty should be dropped entirely: if ideas are recombinations then no one is original in the way the word implies, which is the deflation in [[Originality Is Overrated]].
