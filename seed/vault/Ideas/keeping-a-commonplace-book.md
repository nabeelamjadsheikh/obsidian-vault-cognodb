The practice is old and mostly the same across four centuries: a book in which you copy the passages worth keeping, arranged so you can find them again. Locke wrote a manual on the indexing. Montaigne's essays grew out of one. Darwin ran several.

Seneca's letter on the subject is still the sharpest statement of the danger. We should be like bees, he says: gather from many flowers, but then produce honey. Whatever we have absorbed should not be left whole, or it will remain someone else's; it must be digested, or it will nourish the memory and not the mind.

That is the failure mode of every collection system, ancient or software. A file of beautiful quotations is a monument to other people's thinking, and it feels productive to build because the gathering is pleasant and the digesting is not. My longer notes on the letters are in [[Notes on Letters from a Stoic]] — the reading advice is remarkably modern, including his warning against reading widely and shallowly.

Two disciplines keep it honest for me. The passage never goes in alone: it goes in with a sentence about why I disagree, what it explains, or what it contradicts elsewhere in the vault. And I only gather against a live question, which is the argument in [[Collecting With Intent]] — without one, collecting is hoarding with better typography.

The distinction that makes it usable is that the commonplace book is capture and not composition. It is a notebook, and treating it as a draft ruins both, per [[Notebooks Are Not Drafts]].

What it is actually for is the slow accumulation of things that will one day sit next to each other and react, which is the case for keeping [[The Idea Compost Heap]] rather than a tidy archive.
