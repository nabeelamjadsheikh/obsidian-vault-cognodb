The smallest thing a real person can actually use beats any amount of infrastructure, and it is not close.

The failure mode is seductive because it looks like diligence. You need auth eventually, so you build auth. You need a settings system eventually, so you build a settings system. Six weekends later you have a well-factored application that does nothing, and the thing you can never get back is the information you would have had from watching someone use the ugly version.

A slice is vertical. It touches storage, logic and interface, and at the end of it a person who is not you can do one useful thing. Everything else is scaffolding, and scaffolding is only justified by the building it holds up.

## Why finishing the slice matters more than its quality

Herbert Simon's satisficing is the right stopping rule here. There is no optimal version to find, because the search cost exceeds the value of the difference, and the slice's real job is to generate information, not to be good. Stop at good enough on purpose rather than by exhaustion.

Stephen King's version, from the drafting side, is that you finish the draft and then you find out what it was. Same structure: the artefact tells you things that planning cannot. A plan is a hypothesis about a thing; a slice is the thing.

> Sometimes you have to go on when you don't feel like it, and sometimes you're doing good work when it feels like all you're managing is to shovel shit from a sitting position.

The unfinished vertical slice is the mechanism behind [[Why Most Side Projects Die]] — it is the outflow that never opens, in the framing of [[Scope Is a Stock]]. This is now the single process rule we kept out of [[Lantern — March Retro]]: every item names the smallest usable version of itself, and that version is what gets scheduled.
