The standard explanation is that you got bored. I have looked back at seven of my own dead projects and boredom is nowhere in the record. In every case there is a stretch of weeks where I was still working, still interested, still opening the editor — and shipping nothing. The interest outlived the project by months.

What actually happened is arithmetic. Every session added to the list of things that needed doing, and most sessions finished none of them. The remaining-work level rose steadily while I was fully engaged, and it kept rising until the distance between where I was and where done was became so large that opening the project felt like arriving late to something. That is not boredom. That is a stock overflowing, and it is the whole argument in [[Scope Is a Stock]].

The tell is that the death is silent. Nobody quits a side project. There is no decision, no final commit message saying so. There is just a last Tuesday, and then no more Tuesdays. If you had quit, you would remember quitting.

## The two things that prevent it

**Close the outflow problem, not the inflow problem.** Everyone's instinct is discipline about new ideas, and it does not work, because ideas are the fun part and suppressing them kills the motivation you were trying to preserve. Finishing something visible resets the whole system, which is the entire case for [[Ship the Smallest Real Slice]].

**Keep the gap short enough to return to.** Motivation does not precede the work — it is generated by contact with it, which is the mechanism in [[Motivation Follows Action]]. A project you can restart in ten minutes stays alive. One that requires an hour of re-orientation is already dead, it just does not know yet.

Both are cheap. Neither requires wanting it more.
