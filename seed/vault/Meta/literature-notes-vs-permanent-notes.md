Ahrens draws three levels: fleeting notes, literature notes, permanent notes. The middle one is the one people skip and the last one is the one people think they are writing when they are not.

A **literature note** records what the source said. It is in your words but its subject is the book. Its job is to be a faithful, compressed account you can consult without reopening the original. It is filed with the source and it stays there.

A **permanent note** records what you now think. Its subject is an idea, not a text. It can cite the source but it does not belong to it, and it takes its place in the network alongside notes from twenty other books.

The test I use: could this note be linked from a note about something else entirely without seeming out of place? If it can only be reached through the book it came from, it is a literature note wearing the wrong hat.

## Why the skipped step matters

Going straight from reading to permanent notes produces one of two failures. Either you write summaries and call them ideas, or you write nothing because the leap is too large. The literature note is the ramp.

I learned this the expensive way — see [[Why I Stopped Highlighting]], which is the same lesson arriving via a decade of unread yellow text. Highlighting is a fleeting note that never got processed, and processing is where all the value was.

[[Notes on How to Take Smart Notes]] has my disagreements with the rest of the book, but this distinction I have never had cause to doubt. It also constrains what goes in: [[Collecting With Intent]] is the upstream version, deciding what deserves a literature note at all.

Permanent notes are subject to the same discipline as everything else — [[Atomic Notes]], one claim, linkable from anywhere.
