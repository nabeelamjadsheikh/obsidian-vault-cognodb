A tag is a cheap set membership. A link is an expensive claim about a relationship between two specific ideas. Treating them as interchangeable is the fastest way to produce a graph that looks impressive and tells you nothing.

The asymmetry is in what they cost to create and what they return. Adding `#attention` to a note takes a second and asserts almost nothing — only that this note is somewhere in the neighbourhood. Writing "this is why [[Deliberate Practice]] fails without feedback" costs a sentence of actual thought and asserts something specific and falsifiable. The second is worth a hundred of the first.

## The rules I settled on

- **Tags are for filtering, never for meaning.** If a tag is doing argumentative work, it should have been a link to a note.
- **A tag with one member is noise.** A tag with two hundred is also noise. Somewhere between five and forty is a set worth having.
- **Links must sit in sentences.** A bare "see also" list is a tag pretending to be a link — it has the cost of a link and the information content of a tag.
- **Never tag what you can link.** Topic-shaped tags multiply; ideas do not.

The last one has a corollary worth stating: when a tag starts wanting a definition, it is trying to become a note. Promote it.

Both mechanisms are downstream of [[Links Over Folders]] — having given up the single-location commitment, you need overlapping sets and directed claims, and they do different jobs. [[Maps of Content]] is the third mechanism, and it is neither: a curated ordering that no tag query and no link traversal can produce.

The full ruleset for this vault, including the tag vocabulary, lives in [[Vault Conventions]]. And a link only carries a claim if it points at one thing, which is why [[Atomic Notes]] is upstream of all of this.
