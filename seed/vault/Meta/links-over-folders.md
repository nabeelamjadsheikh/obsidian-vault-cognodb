A folder demands one answer to the question *where does this belong*. Most ideas do not have one answer. [[Anger Is a Reinforcing Loop]] is Stoicism and it is systems thinking, and any filing decision I make about it destroys half of what makes it interesting.

Links do not have this problem, because a note can be linked from anywhere and nothing has to be given up. That is the entire argument, and everything else is administration.

I still keep folders. They are a coarse sort — Philosophy, Meta, Writing — and they answer exactly one question: roughly what neighbourhood is this in. They are not the retrieval mechanism and I do not put effort into them. Getting that boundary right is most of [[Vault Conventions]].

## What replaces the folder's job

Folders were doing two things badly at once: grouping and finding. Split them.

- Grouping is what tags do, cheaply and in overlapping sets. Where that line sits is [[Tags Versus Links]].
- Finding is what curated entry points do. [[Maps of Content]] are hand-written and ordered, which makes them better than any automatic index, because the ordering carries an argument.

The cost of going link-first is real and I do not want to soften it. You lose the ability to answer "what have I got on X" by opening a directory, and you gain the problem described in [[The Index Problem]]: a vault large enough to be useful has too many entry points to remember. That is a genuine regression, and it needs an explicit solution rather than an assumption that search will cover it.

The compensating benefit only shows up at scale. Under two hundred notes, folders are fine. Past that, every folder decision I made early turns out to have been wrong, and the links do not.

None of this works on notes that argue three things at once, because a link to such a note asserts nothing specific — which is why [[Atomic Notes]] is the precondition for going link-first rather than a separate preference.
