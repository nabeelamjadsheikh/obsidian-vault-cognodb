A map of content is a note whose body is mostly links, written by hand, arranged in an order that means something. It is the answer to the question that neither search nor tags can handle: *where do I start*.

The obvious objection is that a machine could generate this. It could — most tools will produce a list of every note tagged `stoicism` in milliseconds, sorted however you like. That list is worse, and the reason is worth being precise about.

**The ordering is the argument.** When I write a map of the Stoicism notes, I put the dichotomy of control first because everything else depends on it, then the exercises that follow from it, then the objections. That sequence is a claim about the structure of the subject. An alphabetical list makes no claim. A backlink count makes a claim about popularity, which is not the same thing and is usually wrong.

**The omissions are also the argument.** A good map leaves things out. A generated index cannot, because it has no basis for exclusion.

## How I keep them

- One map per area, no more than about thirty links, prose between the sections rather than a bare list.
- Rewritten rather than appended to. When a map gets long it is describing an area that has split.
- Linked from nothing much. Maps are entry points; they do not need to be reachable by traversal.

This is the practical answer to [[The Index Problem]], and it is what makes the bet in [[Links Over Folders]] safe to take — you give up the directory listing and get back something that actually orients you. [[Vault Conventions]] treats maps as the one structural thing worth real maintenance time.

Maps are also where [[The Vault as a Thinking Partner]] becomes visible, because writing one forces you to notice which areas have gone quiet.
