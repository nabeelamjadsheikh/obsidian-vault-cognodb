There is exactly one reliable signal, and I spent two years using worse ones before I found it.

**Split when you want to link to only half of it.**

That is the whole test. You are writing some other note, you reach for a link, and you find yourself wanting to say "the second part of that note, not the first". The note has just told you where its seam is. Everything else — word count, number of headings, how it looks in the sidebar — is a proxy that fires on the wrong cases.

The bad signals, for the record:

- **Length.** Some single ideas take five hundred words. Some contain three ideas in ninety.
- **Multiple headings.** Structure within one argument is fine and often good.
- **It feels sprawling.** Feels-like is unreliable and biased toward splitting, because splitting is pleasant and produces the sensation of progress.

The reason the link test works is that it measures the property that actually matters. [[Atomic Notes]] argues that atomicity is a linkability constraint, not a length constraint; the split test is just that constraint observed in the wild rather than guessed at in advance. You do not have to predict which contexts a note will be pulled into. You wait until one arrives and shows you.

There is a symmetric case that nobody talks about: merge when you always link to both. Two notes that are never cited independently are one note with an unnecessary boundary, and boundaries cost traversal.

In practice both checks happen in [[The Weekly Note Hygiene Review]], where oversized notes get the test applied deliberately rather than opportunistically. Splitting is also the main way an ageing note gets rewritten rather than abandoned, which is the [[Evergreen Notes]] discipline in action, and the resulting pieces only make sense in a vault that is [[Links Over Folders]] — otherwise you have just created a filing problem.
