Here is the trap. A vault small enough to hold in your head does not need an index. A vault large enough to be worth having is too large to remember the entry points into. There is no size at which the problem is absent — it is either invisible or acute.

Search does not solve it, and this is the thing people get wrong most often. Search answers *where is the note about X*, which requires you to already know X is in there. The valuable case is the opposite: you have forgotten that you ever wrote about X, and therefore you will never search for it. Full-text search is structurally incapable of surfacing what you have forgotten you know.

Nor does the link graph solve it on its own. Links are excellent at traversal and useless at entry. Once you are somewhere, they take you somewhere better; they do not tell you where to start.

## What actually works

- A small number of hand-written entry points, deliberately maintained. That is [[Maps of Content]], and the argument for writing them by hand rather than generating them is that the ordering encodes a judgement no generator has.
- Tags used as coarse sets, so *everything I have on attention* is a real query rather than an act of recall. The line between that and linking is [[Tags Versus Links]].
- Accepting that some notes will be lost. Not every note needs to be reachable.

Luhmann's own index was surprisingly small — a few hundred terms pointing at one or two cards each. He was betting on traversal for the rest, which is a bet [[Links Over Folders]] makes too, and which only pays if the entry points are good.
