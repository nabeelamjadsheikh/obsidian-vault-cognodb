"One idea per note" gets read as "keep notes short", and that misreading has produced a great many vaults full of useless fragments. Length is not the constraint. Linkability is.

A note is atomic when everything in it is something you would want to point at simultaneously. If half of it could plausibly be the target of a link and the other half could not, it is two notes wearing one filename. That test is the whole rule, and it is the subject of [[When to Split a Note]].

Some atomic notes are three sentences. Some are four hundred words with a heading and a list, because the idea genuinely has that much structure. The systems-thinking notes in this vault are long and still atomic; several of the writing-craft ones are short and are not, because they smuggle two claims in under one title.

## Why the constraint pays

The value of a note is roughly the number of contexts it can be pulled into. A note that argues one thing can be cited by anything that needs that thing. A note that argues three things can only be cited by something that needs all three, which is nothing.

This is why atomicity and [[Links Over Folders]] are the same commitment viewed from different sides. Both refuse to pre-commit an idea to one location. Ahrens is good on this in [[Notes on How to Take Smart Notes]], though he inherits Luhmann's index-card format and I think some of the brevity advice is an artefact of the physical medium rather than the method.

The other half is that atomic notes have to be maintained, not just created. [[Evergreen Notes]] is the practice of coming back and re-cutting them once you know which half you actually wanted to link to.
