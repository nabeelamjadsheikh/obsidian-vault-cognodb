Luhmann published seventy-odd books. Asked how, he pointed at a wooden cabinet and said he did not think alone — he thought with the slip box, which he called a second memory and treated as a partner in conversation.

In *Communicating with Slip Boxes* he is explicit that the productivity came from a property most people optimise away. The box was not organised by topic. New cards went behind whichever existing card they were a response to, regardless of subject, and were given a branching number to fix the position. Over decades this produced a structure nobody designed and nobody could have designed.

## Why the lack of organisation is the point

A topic hierarchy can only return what you already knew to look for. A structure that grew from local decisions — *this card responds to that one* — accumulates adjacencies that reflect the actual history of your thinking rather than a taxonomy imposed in advance. Those adjacencies are where the surprises live, which is the whole case in [[Why I Keep a Zettelkasten]].

What made it navigable was not the structure but a small set of hand-maintained entry points into it. That is the real lesson and it is the subject of [[The Index Problem]] — his index was thin, deliberately, and pointed at one or two cards per topic on the assumption that the links would carry you the rest of the way.

The romanticism around this needs some pushback. *Social Systems* is famously unreadable, and the theory of it is not obviously improved by having been assembled from cards. But the working relationship he describes — being genuinely answered by his own archive — is real and reproducible, and it is what I am after in [[The Vault as a Thinking Partner]].
